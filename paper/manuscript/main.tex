\begin{abstract}
Language models are now routinely proposed as the high-level planner for robot
fleets, calling a library of skills as tools. They are evaluated on
homogeneous fleets, with idealized execution, on runs that cannot be repeated.
We present Ripple, a multi-agent simulator in which fixed-wing aircraft and
multirotors share one world and any episode, the model's replies included, can
be replayed exactly. Fleet planning and vehicle execution are separate
plugins, and each vehicle's autonomy hosts a library of skills. With it we
measure, on the same episodes, whether a planner follows instructions it was
never programmed for, whether offering it the right skill helps it comply,
and how often it gives a vehicle a task its dynamics rule out. The task is
medical delivery by a fleet of fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors. A
planner that breaks a rule in fewer than one decision in ten still breaks it
in most missions, because a mission gives it many chances; for two of three
rules, which of two plain wordings is used decides whether it does, and the
right skill or a check in code removes the breaches. Without the vehicles'
limits in the prompt, it gives vehicles tasks they cannot fly. All results
are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robot foundation models split the work in two: a slow planner decides in
language what to do next, and a fast policy does it. $\pi_{0.5}$ predicts
``pick up the pillow'' from ``clean the bedroom'' before producing motor
commands \cite{black2025pi05}, and GR00T N1 pairs a reasoning model with a
fast action model in the same way \cite{nvidia2025gr00t}. The same split
suits a fleet of vehicles. A language model reads requests and decides who
does what, and each vehicle's autonomy and autopilot carry it out. Language
models have been proposed for exactly this
\cite{li2026llmmrs,kannan2024smartllm,liu2025coherent,zhang2026multiuavplat}.

The planner's actions are calls to skills: fly a route, drop a package, land,
loiter. These are solved classical behaviors. Consider a fleet delivering
blood to clinics, as drones do in Rwanda \cite{nisingizwe2022drone}. An urgent
request arrives for a distant clinic, and another for a hospital rooftop
nearby. A fixed-wing aircraft covers the long leg fastest and drops its
package on a pass, but it cannot hover, has a minimum speed, and needs room to
turn; only a multirotor can land on the roof. Existing planners describe a
robot's abilities mainly as a list of discrete skills
\cite{kannan2024smartllm,liu2025coherent}, which says little about these
limits. Operations also change the rules mid-mission: a clinic closes, a
school's airspace is off limits for an hour, a vehicle is grounded. A fixed
optimizer cannot take such a rule until an engineer codes it; a language model
can read it at once. \emph{Does it follow rules nobody programmed, and does it
know what each vehicle can do?}

A rule is not kept once. A fleet's planner decides again whenever a request
arrives or a vehicle comes home, and while a rule is in force it may decide a
dozen times or more, each time with a chance to break it. A planner that
keeps a rule in nineteen decisions out of twenty looks dependable when it is
tested a decision at a time, yet over a mission with fifteen such chances it
breaks the rule more often than not. Rules also compete with the task: a
rule is tested hardest at the moment keeping it would make a delivery late.
Whether a planner follows rules nobody programmed is a question about whole
missions, not single replies.

Answering this is harder than it looks, because the evaluations do not repeat.
A model at temperature zero still gives different replies from run to run,
enough to move accuracy by up to 15 points
\cite{he2025nondeterminism,atil2024nondeterminism}, and the fleet studies
closest to ours report a handful of flights or a few runs per configuration
\cite{ludwig2026cola,iannoli2026webofdrones,zhang2026multiuavplat}. In Ripple
every random number is tied to what it is for, and the model's replies are
saved, so a whole episode can be replayed exactly. Two planners can then be
compared on the same episodes, and a planner's own variation measured apart
from the world's.

In this paper, we build an engine for answering these questions. Ripple is
a multi-agent simulator in which fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors share
one world, each vehicle's autonomy hosts the skills the planner calls, and a
whole mission, the model's replies included, replays exactly. Instructions
arrive mid-mission in plain language, and whether they were kept is checked
against the trajectories the fleet actually flew, not against the plan. A
mission can be replayed without contacting a model, run again with only the
rule's wording or the skills on offer changed, or stopped at any decision and
asked again. Optimizers handle rules well when every rule is coded in advance
\cite{choudhury2020scoba}, and FLEET turns instructions given up front into
a schedule for a solver \cite{rivera2025fleet}; we look at rules that arrive
mid-mission, and compare the planner with itself on the same mission without
the rule.

We use Ripple to study a mixed fleet delivering medical supplies under rules
nobody programmed. Asked one decision at a time, Claude Haiku breaks a rule
in at most one reply in ten. Over whole missions, for two of three rules, one
plain wording is never broken while another, equally plain, is broken in 8
and 9 of 10 missions, and a no-fly zone is broken in 6 of 10 under either
wording; the per-decision rates predict how many missions break. The breaks
lean toward urgent deliveries. In the mission of Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}, the
planner holds an urgent delivery for six minutes rather than fly through a
no-fly zone, then sends an aircraft straight through with 1.6 minutes to
spare. Offering a skill that routes around the zone removed every no-fly
breach, and refusing breaking calls in code removed every breach, each at
little cost in deliveries. Told nothing of the vehicles' limits, the planner
sends fixed-wing aircraft to land about 18 times a mission.

Recent work delegates a growing share of a robot's decisions to language
models, on the premise that their general knowledge covers situations that
no one specified in advance \cite{li2026llmmrs,black2025pi05}. Our initial
results suggest that this premise should be tested before it is relied on.
Told a safety rule in plain language, the planner keeps
it most of the time but not reliably, and unless the edge case is spelled out
in the prompt or guarded in code, it can make the kind of mistake real
operations treat as serious, such as flying through a no-fly zone to make a
deadline. Our contributions are Ripple, an engine for finding such mistakes
before deployment, in which language-model fleet planners are evaluated on
exactly replayable missions with mixed fleets and rules judged from the
flight, and a first study made with it. We also measure whether the planner
gives a vehicle a task its dynamics rule out.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language models as planners for robot teams}
Language models can plan for teams of robots: they split tasks by skill,
negotiate among robots, and coordinate heterogeneous teams of quadrotors,
ground robots, and arms through rounds of feedback
\cite{kannan2024smartllm,mandi2024roco,chen2024scalable,liu2025coherent,yang2025autohmallm,li2026llmmrs}.
For aerial fleets they turn operator commands into calls to ArduPilot and
PX4, compose swarm choreographies behind a safety filter, and are
benchmarked on more than a thousand tasks with flight control abstracted
away
\cite{iannoli2026webofdrones,ludwig2026cola,nazzari2025tacos,schuck2025swarmgpt,zhang2026multiuavplat}.
Larger aircraft bring their own demands, from take-off and landing
procedures to regulation \cite{yuan2025nelv}, and coordination of fixed-wing
swarms by language models is under study \cite{amass2025}. However, these
planners are judged on the plan they return for a task given at the start, a
robot's abilities are described as a list of discrete skills, and each drone
fleet is of a single type. A fleet that mixes fixed-wing aircraft, which
cannot hover and must keep flying, with multirotors raises a different
question: whether the planner respects what each vehicle can do. Rules also
change in operation, and a plan made at the start cannot show whether they
are kept. We give the planner a mixed fleet and rules that arrive
mid-mission, tell it each vehicle's limits, and judge it from the fleet's
flight over the whole mission.

\paragraph{Language models over skills}
A language model can drive a robot through a library of described skills:
it chooses among them, writes programs that call them, grows the library,
or hands the hard part to a classical planner or an external verifier
\cite{ahn2022saycan,liang2023codeaspolicies,wang2023voyager,wang2024robogen,liu2023llmp,chen2023autotamp,kambhampati2024llmmodulo}.
Documentation alone is enough for a model to use a new tool
\cite{hsieh2023tooldoc}, which makes adding a skill cheap. For software
agents, a study of more than a thousand skills finds that picking the right
one gets harder as the library grows, and that errors across a chain of
picks multiply \cite{chen2026skillscaling}. However, these studies ask
whether the model reaches a goal with the skills it has. Which skills are
offered is treated as a fixed part of the setup, not as something that
shapes what the model does. Choosing skills is well studied; we show that
which skills are offered also decides whether a planner keeps a rule, and
that a skill that makes the right action easy can do as much as a
better-worded rule.

\paragraph{Following rules}
Rules that change while an agent works have so far been studied in text
worlds. House Rules attaches an editable specification to tasks in ALFWorld,
CookingWorld, and ScienceWorld and revises it mid-task through timed
natural-language updates; the best agent breaks an active constraint in 15\%
of episodes, more often as the update is worded less directly (from 2\% to
28\%), and a privileged symbolic shield removes every violation without
changing task success \cite{vimalkanth2026houserules}. For robot agents, the
Safety Chip turns natural-language rules into linear temporal logic, prunes
actions that would break them, and tells the model why so that it can
replan, in VirtualHome and on a real robot \cite{yang2024safetychip}.
Reliability also falls as an exchange grows: models that do well when a task
is given in one turn do much worse, and far less consistently, when the same
task arrives over several \cite{laban2025lost}. However, these studies judge
rules against an agent's actions in text or household simulators, and report
violations mainly per episode. A fleet is different: a rule is broken by
where a vehicle flies, not by a named action, and the planner decides many
times while a rule is in force. We check rules against the trajectories a
fleet actually flew and relate slips per decision to whole missions: small
per-decision rates add up, two equally plain wordings of a rule can differ,
and a skill, not only a filter, can close the gap.

\paragraph{Reproducible evaluation}
Results in learning swing with the seeds and evaluation choices behind them,
so careful work reports intervals over many runs
\cite{agarwal2021precipice,gorsane2022evaluation}. Agent benchmarks have
reached the same point. On SWE-bench, single-run scores of the same agent
vary by several points even at temperature zero, because trajectories
diverge within the first few percent of tokens \cite{bjarnason2026randomness};
$\tau$-bench's pass$^k$ shows that agents which solve a task once often fail
when the same task is repeated \cite{yao2024taubench}; and a model at
temperature zero gives different replies from run to run
\cite{he2025nondeterminism,atil2024nondeterminism}. Software-agent tools
respond by recording model calls and replaying them: Inspect caches model
output during evaluation \cite{aisi2024inspect}, and Chronicle records a run
at its nondeterministic boundaries so that it replays without model calls
\cite{chawla2026chronicle}. However, a saved reply reproduces a run only if
everything that shapes the prompt is deterministic. Robot simulators promise
that for one version on one machine at best \cite{mujocodocs,isaaclabdocs},
and GPU simulators can give different results across parallel environments
\cite{zhang2026gpusimbench}. In Ripple the whole episode, simulation and
model calls together, replays exactly, so the same mission can be rerun with
one thing changed, and the unreliability that pass$^k$ finds across repeated
trials shows up within a single mission, over its many decisions.

Our task is medical delivery by a mixed fleet (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}). Two
fixed-wing aircraft and two multirotors share one depot, which serves six
clinics 3--13~km away and two hospital rooftops nearby. The fixed-wing
aircraft are fast and fly the long legs, dropping packages by parachute on a
pass; the multirotors are slower but can hover, so they can also land, and
only they can serve a hospital rooftop. Fixed-wing aircraft fly a kinematic
model with a minimum speed and a limited turn rate under a PID autopilot,
and multirotors a point model capped at 12~m/s. Over a 30-minute mission,
twenty requests open, some of them urgent, and the planner decides which
vehicle takes each one. To check that the results do not hinge on the
kinematic model, we repeat the no-fly missions with the fixed-wing aircraft
on a 6-DOF model under its autopilot (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).

The planner never flies a vehicle itself. It calls skills, such as drop,
land, or drop by way of a waypoint, and each vehicle's autonomy carries out
the call over that vehicle's own controller and dynamics. Whenever a request
arrives or a vehicle comes home, the planner sees the open requests, each
vehicle's state and limits (minimum speed, turn radius, whether it can
hover, how much flight time it has left), the skills it may use, and any
instruction in force, and it replies with calls. Each decision is a new
prompt built from the mission as it stands, without the planner's earlier
decisions (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt} shows one in full). This keeps memory
out of the test. Every rule in force appears in every prompt, so when the
planner breaks a rule, the rule was in front of it, and the loss of
reliability over long exchanges \cite{laban2025lost} plays no part. It also
means each prompt depends only on the mission's state, so a reply saved once
is reused on every rerun and the mission replays exactly. A call a vehicle
cannot carry out, such as sending a fixed-wing aircraft to land on a roof, is
refused and counted.

Partway through the mission, operations may send one instruction in plain
language: a clinic closes, a no-fly zone opens, a multirotor has to stay on
standby, or a vehicle is grounded. Nothing enforces it; the planner alone
decides whether to follow it. We write each instruction several ways and
check, from what the fleet actually flew, whether it was obeyed.

We score a mission by the share of requests delivered on time, and report
broken instructions, refused calls, and vehicles lost for lack of endurance
beside it. Every comparison is made on the same missions.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:system}

Each agent is a stack of plugins: an autonomy that decides what to do, a
controller that turns that into commands, a motion model that integrates the vehicle's
dynamics, and sensors, with interaction, network, and metrics plugins acting
on the whole world. Following SCRIMMAGE \cite{demarco2018}, from which
Ripple was ported, each of these is selected by name in a mission file, so a
study can swap one part without touching the rest. An autonomy plugin keeps
its own state and sets its vehicle's commands every tick, so one plugin can
carry several behaviors and switch among them; ours holds the library of
skills the planner calls (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}), each skill one file of
60--120 lines. Each tick runs the phases in a fixed order, and each phase
finishes for every agent before the next begins
(Appendix~\ref{app:self}).

On this base, Ripple adds what a study of language-model planners needs.
Plugins pass typed values with units and frames, checked where they connect,
so the same skill can drive a fixed-wing aircraft or a multirotor, whichever
motion model the mission picks. Every random number is tied to what it is for
(seed, agent, plugin, tick, quantity) rather than to the order it was drawn,
so a run gives the same result however it is parallelized, and two runs of the
same episode meet the same wind and the same events. With the planner's
replies saved, an experiment reruns exactly without contacting a model. Sweeps
over any parameter and seed are built in and run in parallel.

\prov A 60~s mission with three Rascals on the 6-DOF model under their
autopilots, at 100 steps per second, runs in about 40~ms on one core of an
Apple M2 Pro, including writing the recording.

Each ported model is checked against SCRIMMAGE. Over 100 generated scenarios
the two agree to $2.8\times10^{-11}$~m, and the fixed-wing aircraft and its
autopilot agree to $2.3\times10^{-13}$~m. This shows the equations and
timing came through intact, not that they match a real aircraft.


\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

The questions in this paper are about whole missions, so we run every
condition as a full 30-minute mission and judge it from what the fleet flew.
The wordings and the scoring were built on three development missions; the
results below come from five new test missions that were not used to build
them, each run with two independent replies from Claude Haiku. Every reply
is saved, so every result reruns exactly. Two references frame the
comparisons: the same missions without the instruction, for what keeping a
rule costs, and the planner with breaking calls refused in code, which keeps
every rule by construction. Our experiments focus on the following
questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Does the planner follow rules nobody programmed, and what does
  following them cost?
\item When it slips, does the right skill help more than better wording or
  a check in code?
\item Does the planner give a vehicle a task that vehicle can't fly?
\end{enumerate}
As a control, we also measure how much a planner's score varies between live
reruns of the same missions. \stub{More models, including an open-weights
one, and the number of prompt versions tried.}

\subsection{Does the planner follow rules nobody programmed?}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig-slope.tex}
\caption{\textbf{Dependable one decision at a time, not over a mission.} For
each rule and wording, the share of decisions that broke the rule where it
could be broken (left) and the share of the ten missions broken (right), on
one scale; with ten missions each, the right-hand shares are good to about 25
to 30 points (95\% intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:counts}). Slips in 7--10\% of decisions break 60--90\%
of missions. For the grounded vehicle and the standby rule, wording A was
never broken while wording B, as plain, almost always was ($p < 0.001$
each, Fisher's exact test on missions broken); the no-fly zone was broken as
often under either wording.}
\label{fig:slips}
\end{figure}

To answer Question 1, we gave the planner three of the instructions (the
no-fly zone, the grounded vehicle, and the multirotor on standby), each in
two wordings: the one written first (A), and the one broken most often in
development (B). Clinic closures were never broken in development and are left
out. Each mission is compared with the same mission run without the
instruction. To see how the planner does one decision at a time, we also
took ten moments per rule from the test missions at which the rule could be
broken, kept each prompt exactly, swapped in each wording, and asked again,
twice per moment.

Fig.~\ref{fig:slips} shows the results; the counts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:counts}. One decision at a time, the planner looks
dependable: it broke a rule in at most one reply in ten (no replies under
wording A for the grounded vehicle and the standby rule, 2 of 20 under B, and
1 of 20 for the no-fly zone under either). Over whole missions, it is not. A
mission offers 7 to 22 decisions at which a rule can be broken, and if each
slipped independently at the measured rate $p$, a mission with $n$ of them
would be broken with probability $1-(1-p)^n$; this predicts the observed share
to within two missions in ten, a little low for the rules that offer the most
chances. For the grounded vehicle and the multirotor on standby, wording A was
kept in every decision of every mission, while wording B, just as plain, was
broken in 8 and 9 of 10 missions ($p < 0.001$ for each, Fisher's exact test on
missions broken). The no-fly zone was broken in 6 of 10 missions under either
wording. We suspect the reason is what the rule asks of the planner: the
other two name the vehicle or the act to avoid, while the no-fly rule names
only a place, and keeping it means working out that a straight route to a
clinic crosses that place, which neither wording does for it. The skill that
makes the route explicit removed every no-fly breach (Sec.~\ref{sec:skill}),
which fits this reading, but we have not tested it directly. Breaks lean toward urgent deliveries: 32 of the 37 breaking calls
served an urgent request, against two thirds of the calls that kept a rule,
with a median of 7 minutes to spare against 10. Following a rule also costs
deliveries: the grounded vehicle's wording A, kept in every mission, cost the
most, 10 points of on-time share.

\subsection{Does the right skill help the planner comply?}
\label{sec:skill}
To answer Question 2, we offered the planner \texttt{drop\_via}, a drop flown
out and back through a waypoint it chooses, alongside \texttt{drop} and
\texttt{land} under the no-fly zone, and compared the same missions without
it and with breaking calls refused in code. With \texttt{drop\_via} offered,
no decision broke the rule under either wording (0 of 53 each), against 8 of
84 and 7 of 73 without it, and no mission was broken against 6 of 10
($p = 0.011$ for each wording), at about the same on-time share (0.50 and
0.47, against 0.46 and 0.48). Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the call in
the mission of Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}: offered the skill, the planner routes
the urgent delivery around the zone the moment it arrives. Refusing breaking
calls in code removed every breach of every rule, refusing 26 calls, at
little cost. A skill that makes the right action easy did as much as the
better wording, and for the no-fly zone, where no wording worked, more.
\stub{Planned: remove skills, and vary how they are described.}

\subsection{Does the planner know what each vehicle can do?}
To answer Question 3, we count calls that give a vehicle a task it cannot
fly, with the vehicles' limits stated in the prompt and left out, in the
missions without an instruction. With the limits stated, no call over ten
missions sent a vehicle on a skill it cannot run. With them left out, the
check refused 179 calls that sent a fixed-wing aircraft to land, about 18 a
mission, and the on-time share fell from 0.50 to 0.41. The planner needs to
be told what each vehicle can do.

With the fixed-wing aircraft on the 6-DOF model and its autopilot, the no-fly
zone was still broken, in 2 and 5 of 10 missions under wordings A and B,
against 6 and 6 on the kinematic model, at similar on-time shares (0.45 and
0.46, against 0.46 and 0.48). Neither difference is significant ($p = 0.17$
and $p = 1.0$): the breaches do not depend on the simpler model. The
multirotors stay on a point model; a multirotor's path is close to the
straight line the point model flies, the fixed-wing aircraft is the vehicle
whose path departs from it most, and the grounding and standby rules depend
on which vehicle flies, not how.

\subsection{Is a planner's score noise?}
As a control, we reran the same missions live. The world is identical
between reruns, so any difference comes from the model. \prov In the first
pilot, three live reruns of Claude Haiku over ten missions differed by 0.008
in mean on-time share. \stub{Repeat on the test missions.}

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

\stub{What the experiments show, in a few sentences.} So far: judging
a planner one decision at a time understates how often a live mission goes
wrong.

All results are from simulation. The models are checked against independent
implementations, not flight data. Flying the same planners and skills on a few
small quadrotors is the natural next step. A skill's interface also admits a
learned version beside the classical one, and re-running the tasks of a recent
drone study \cite{iannoli2026webofdrones} on mixed fleets would test whether
its results hold under replay.

\section{One Decision in Full}
\label{app:prompt}

Below is the decision of Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}, word for word: everything
the planner saw and its reply. At minute 3.6 an urgent request opens for
clinic E, and the straight route there crosses a no-fly zone that starts at
minute 5. A fixed-wing aircraft flying straight would still be inside the
zone when it starts. The planner instead calls \texttt{drop\_via} with a
waypoint at (1, 5)~km, east of the zone, and the aircraft delivers without
entering it.
In the mission of Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}, which offers no such skill, the
planner held the same request and later flew straight through.

stands; nothing from earlier decisions is included. Long lines are wrapped.
