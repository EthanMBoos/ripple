\begin{abstract}
Methods for multi-robot coordination are chosen largely by their rank on
simulated benchmarks, where agents are points, observations are exact, and
messages always arrive. Learned methods are mostly ranked against one another
there, and when they are compared with classical methods, the two often receive
different information. Whether these rankings survive the sensing noise, message
loss, latency, and vehicle dynamics that real hardware produces, and so whether
they choose the right method, has not been measured. We measure it in Ripple, a
deterministic simulator built for controlled sweeps, in which learned and
classical policies receive the same information and commands and every model is
verified against an independent implementation or published measurements.
Starting from cooperative navigation exactly as a standard benchmark defines it,
we sweep each disturbance from none to beyond what real hardware produces, on
point agents, a multirotor, and fixed-wing aircraft, across navigation,
collision avoidance, and flocking, and measure how the gap between the learned
and classical methods changes. \textit{[Finding: how each gap changes across its
sweep, whether and where it changes sign relative to the realistic range, and
which methods degrade gradually and which fail abruptly.]} \textit{[Finding: how
far retraining under realistic conditions shifts the gap.]} All comparisons are
between simulations; they describe the modeled effects under the tested
conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Multi-robot teams are meant to work outside the lab: quadrotors mapping a
building, fixed-wing aircraft searching a coastline, ground vehicles sharing
a warehouse. In each, the team's sensing is imperfect, its radios drop and
delay messages, and its vehicles cannot move however a planner wishes. Yet
the methods these teams run are mostly chosen in simulators that leave all of
this out. Multi-agent reinforcement learning is developed on benchmarks built
for speed, such as the particle environments \cite{lowe2017maddpg}, VMAS
\cite{bettini2022vmas}, and JaxMARL \cite{rutherford2023jaxmarl}, where agents
are points, observations are exact, and messages always arrive. Methods are
ranked there, and the ranking guides which ones are built on and deployed.
\emph{Does a ranking measured on such a benchmark still hold once sensing,
communication, and the vehicle become realistic?}

Some degradation is expected as noise, loss, and dynamics are added. What
matters for choosing a method is whether they hurt one method more than
another, and by how much. Consider a team covering a set of landmarks. A
classical planner assigns each agent to a landmark and flies it there; with
exact positions, the assignment minimizes total travel. With noisy
positions, two agents at similar distances may swap targets back and forth.
One possible outcome is that the planner collapses at a particular noise
level while a learned policy that reacts to each observation degrades
smoothly. Whether the benchmark picked the right method then depends on
where that collapse falls relative
to the noise that real positioning systems produce. The question therefore
calls for a curve, not a single comparison: for each disturbance, how the gap
between the two methods changes as the disturbance grows, and where the
realistic range lies along it.

We measure these curves with Ripple, a deterministic multi-robot simulator
built for controlled sweeps (Fig.~1). In Ripple, learned and classical
policies read the same belief, built from the same sensors and messages, and
command the same controllers, so neither side receives information the other
lacks, a confound common in published comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:tasks}). Every run can be repeated exactly, so both methods
are evaluated on identical episodes, and each physical effect can be switched
on alone. Each model the study relies on is checked against an independent
implementation or published measurements.

The study starts from cooperative navigation exactly as a standard benchmark
defines it: Ripple reproduces the benchmark's motion step by step, so the
starting ranking can be checked by anyone. From there, both methods switch to
the velocity command a real vehicle takes, and we sweep sensing noise,
message loss, latency, and, for the aircraft, wind, each from none to well
beyond what real hardware produces, on point agents, a Crazyflie quadrotor,
and fixed-wing aircraft, across navigation, collision avoidance, and
flocking. For each sweep we report the gap curve over ten training seeds,
whether and where it changes sign relative to the realistic range, and the
failures behind it.
Finally, we train the learned policies under realistic conditions to see how
far that shifts their curves.

Our central contribution is this measurement: gap curves between learned and
classical multi-robot methods across realistic disturbances and vehicles,
starting from a benchmark's own task, with both methods given the same
information and commands. It rests on a second contribution, Ripple, in
which vehicle models, sensing, communication, and external autopilots share
one deterministic pipeline and each model is checked against an outside
source. \stub{Summarize the findings: how each gap changed across its sweep,
any changes of sign and where they fall relative to the realistic range, and
which methods fail abruptly. Do not assume a change of sign before the
experiments show one.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Multi-agent simulators}
PettingZoo standardized how a learner steps a multi-agent environment
\cite{terry2021pettingzoo}, and Isaac Gym, VMAS, and JaxMARL moved simulation
onto the GPU
\cite{makoviychuk2021isaacgym,bettini2022vmas,rutherford2023jaxmarl}. These
tools are moving toward physical fidelity, with actuator models and sensors
in Isaac Lab \cite{mittal2025isaaclab} and drone swarms in Crazyflow
\cite{schuck2026crazyflow} and MuJoCo-Drones-Gym \cite{tayal2026mujocodrones}.
Communication is usually an action channel or part of the observation; queued
delivery with delay and loss comes from co-simulation with a network simulator
\cite{burmeister2025defiance}. Vehicle dynamics come from dedicated tools such
as JSBSim \cite{berndt2004jsbsim}, the autopilots' own simulators
\cite{ardupilotsitl,px4gazebo}, and RotorS \cite{furrer2016rotors}. These tools
aim at training throughput or at one vehicle's physics. Ripple aims instead at
controlled comparison: one factor changed at a time, with every run repeatable.

\paragraph{Fidelity and transfer}
Several projects train in a fast simulator and test somewhere more
realistic: maritime game policies on uncrewed surface vessels
\cite{crowley2025pyquaticus}, a connected-vehicle benchmark that separates
the loss from a different control stack from the loss from a more realistic
environment \cite{beerwerth2026zeroshot}, and policies moved between aircraft
through per-aircraft controllers \cite{sun2026bvrsim}. Labiosa and Hanna
ablated which simplifications let robot soccer policies transfer, and matched
a well-tuned hand-written behavior \cite{labiosa2025abstract}. Truong et
al.\ found that a lower-fidelity simulator transferred better than a
higher-fidelity one for legged visual navigation
\cite{truong2022rethinking}, so a more detailed simulation is not
automatically closer to reality. These studies ask what makes a policy
transfer. We ask whether added realism reorders methods.

\paragraph{Learned and classical methods across simulators}
MASP compares a MAPPO-trained planner with Hungarian-assignment planners on a
particle navigation task and on simulated quadrotors, and finds the learned
planner faster in both \cite{yang2025masp}. Tolstaya et al.\ trained flocking
controllers on point masses and on quadrotors in AirSim, with classical
controllers as bounds \cite{tolstaya2020gnn}. In both, the environments
differ in several ways at once, and the aim is a better method. Kadian et
al.\ measure how well simulation predicts real-robot results, for a single
agent and learned policies only \cite{kadian2020sim2real}, and Li et al.\
find that robustness of cooperative learning to one perturbation does not
carry over to another \cite{li2025robustness}. Reviews of MARL evaluation
find inconsistent seeds, metrics, and baselines
\cite{gorsane2022evaluation,papoudakis2021benchmarking}, and SMACv2 showed
that a fixed benchmark can be solved without the observations it provides
\cite{ellis2023smacv2}. In contrast, we change one modeled effect at a time,
give learned and classical methods the same information and commands, and
start from a benchmark's own task.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We compare a learned method $L$ and a classical method $C$ on a task with a
primary metric $J$, where higher is better. A sweep varies one disturbance,
such as the standard deviation of position noise, over levels
$0 = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_K$, where $x_0$ is the undisturbed setting.
Each training seed $s$ yields one learned policy. Both methods run the same
$E$ evaluation episodes at every level, and the seed's gap at level $x_k$ is
the learned policy's average lead,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_s(x_k) = \frac{1}{E}\sum_{e=1}^{E}
  \left[ J_{L,s}(x_k, e) - J_C(x_k, e) \right].
\end{equation}
The function $x \mapsto \Delta_s(x)$ is the seed's gap curve, and the gap
curves of ten seeds are the primary result. Published specifications give a
realistic range $R$ for each disturbance. For one effect, the ranking holds
if the sign of $\Delta_s(x_0)$ holds at every level inside $R$. On point
agents, $x_0$ already uses velocity commands; the switch from the
benchmark's force actions is measured as a step of its own, so its effect
is not mixed into any sweep. When the sign changes between neighbouring
levels $x_k$ and $x_{k+1}$, we report the change as bracketed by them, with
the number of seeds that show it. Vehicles do not form a continuous axis, so
each vehicle rung has its own sweeps, and rungs are compared at matching
levels.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:system}

Ripple began as a Rust port of the C++ simulator SCRIMMAGE
\cite{demarco2018} and agrees with SCRIMMAGE to within floating-point
rounding on every compared mission (Table~\ref{tab:checks-main}); known
defects in SCRIMMAGE are fixed in both for the comparison. Four of Ripple's
properties carry the study.

\paragraph{One deterministic tick}
Each tick runs fixed phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:tick}), and every phase finishes
for every agent before the next begins. Output is byte-identical at any
number of CPU workers and with recording on or off, so learned and classical
policies can be evaluated on exactly the same episodes, and any run can be
replayed.

\paragraph{Truth, measurements, and belief}
Sensors read the true state and produce measurements; estimators turn
measurements and received messages into each agent's belief; policies and
controllers read only belief. A disturbance therefore reaches a decision
only through what the agent could have measured or been told, and learned
and classical policies receive identical information.

\paragraph{Controlled sweeps}
Every physical effect is an optional parameter of its model, off by default,
so earlier results stay valid as effects are added. A sweep file varies any
mission parameter across levels and seeds and runs in parallel shards, so a
change in a curve can be attributed to the one factor that changed.

\paragraph{Checked models}
Each model the study uses is checked against an external source
(Table~\ref{tab:checks-main}). Agreement with another implementation shows
that the equations, conversions, and timing came through intact; agreement
with a real vehicle requires measurements, which the Crazyflie's parameters
have and the other models do not yet.

\section{Study Design}
\label{sec:tasks}

\subsection{Vehicles}

\paragraph{Point agents}
The benchmark's point agents (MPE2's particle model, with its damping and
soft contact force) reproduce MPE2 step by step
(Table~\ref{tab:checks-main}). On the ladder of vehicle rungs, the same point
agents follow a velocity command through a fixed controller.

\paragraph{Multirotor}
The main realistic vehicle is a Crazyflie, the quadrotor on which learned
multi-robot policies are most often flown
\cite{batra2021quadswarm,chen2023stgnn,chen2025open}. Its mass, inertia,
drag, and thrust and torque coefficients come from bench measurements of the
real vehicle \cite{forster2015crazyflie}; motor lag, rotor drag, and the
rolling moment follow RotorS \cite{furrer2016rotors}. A cascaded controller
flies it from the velocity command, reaching a 1~m/s step with a 1.0~s rise
and 20\% overshoot at 500~Hz. Its main uncertainty is motor lag: an
independent identification of a similar vehicle gives a lag twice as long
\cite{schuck2026crazyflow}, so the lag is itself worth sweeping.

\paragraph{Fixed wing}
Fixed-wing aircraft are the stress case: they cannot stop, hold a position,
or match a neighbour's velocity at any speed. Two models separate the causes
of a failure. A kinematic aircraft has only a minimum speed and a turning
radius, fitted to the Rascal; a 6-DOF model flies the Rascal's coefficient
tables, with stall tables after JSBSim, under ArduPilot's autopilot run in
lockstep or a native controller. A failure on both points to the inability
to stop; one that appears only with 6-DOF dynamics points to the dynamics,
or to the controller that flies them.
For aircraft, the velocity command becomes a heading and a speed held above
the minimum airspeed, so a command to stop becomes a loiter.

well-established classical method, and each most sensitive to one factor
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks}).

\paragraph{Cooperative navigation}
$N$ agents must cover $N$ landmarks without colliding, as in the particle
environments' \emph{spread} task \cite{lowe2017maddpg}. The classical solution
assigns agents to landmarks optimally \cite{kuhn1955hungarian}, or plans the
assignment and trajectories together \cite{turpin2014capt}. Our baseline
re-solves the assignment at every step from each agent's own belief and
steers the point where the agent would coast to a stop: with the benchmark's
damping, an agent at $p$ with velocity $v$ pushed with force $F$ for one step
$\Delta t$ comes to rest at $p + 4v\Delta t + 4F\Delta t^2$, and the
controller applies the force that puts that point on the landmark, clipped to
the benchmark's limit. Every number comes from the benchmark, so the baseline
has no fitted parameters, and written against the benchmark implementation
it must reproduce Ripple's scores. We report the benchmark return, the
landmarks occupied, collisions, and distance flown against the optimal
assignment's straight-line cost. The main factor is sensing; communication is
not varied, because the benchmark's agents observe one another directly.

\paragraph{Circle-crossing collision avoidance}
Agents start on a circle and fly to the opposite side, so all of them meet
in the middle. The classical method is reciprocal velocity obstacles (ORCA)
\cite{vandenberg2011orca,alonsomora2010nhorca}. Several learned policies are
reported to beat ORCA
\cite{chen2017cadrl,long2018towards,everett2021ga3c,fan2020distributed}, but
the comparisons are less clear than they appear: the median gain was small
\cite{chen2017cadrl}, ORCA's radii were enlarged to absorb discrete-time
collisions \cite{everett2021ga3c}, and a laser-scanner policy was compared
with an ORCA given its neighbours' true states \cite{long2018towards}. Here
both read the same belief, and the main factor is dynamics. Aircraft fly a
holding pattern at the goal. We measure success, collisions, deadlocks, extra
time, and closest approach, including the worst cases.

\paragraph{Flocking with limited communication}
Agents start with random velocities and must align and stay together,
communicating only with neighbours in range. The classical methods are
Reynolds' rules \cite{reynolds1987flocks} and Olfati-Saber's algorithms
\cite{olfatisaber2006flocking}, which have flown outdoors on 30 drones
\cite{vasarhelyi2018optimized}. Learned flocking has been tested under fading
and delay \cite{gao2025airgnn,marino2024gated}, against expert controllers
rather than classical rules. The main factor is communication. Aircraft flock
around a moving reference point. We measure velocity alignment, the number of
separate groups, closest approach, and messages sent.

\subsection{Policies and Training}

Ripple has no training code. Policies are trained with existing libraries
\cite{yu2022mappo,bettini2024benchmarl} in fast simulators
\cite{bettini2022vmas,rutherford2023jaxmarl,terry2021pettingzoo}, exported,
and run inside Ripple as an autonomy plugin with a fixed thread count and
their own random stream, which keeps runs deterministic. To train at the
realistic setting, an outside learner steps Ripple through a standard
multi-agent interface \cite{terry2021pettingzoo}. A parity test checks each
task's observations against the training simulator, and we reproduce one
published result, MAPPO on the spread task \cite{papoudakis2021benchmarking},
with its authors' code, as a check on our training setup. Each learned method
is trained three ways: at the simplest setting, at the realistic setting, and
with the setting randomized. Comparing them separates a policy trained in the
wrong place from a method that is fragile. Classical methods get the same
tuning budget, counted in evaluations; the navigation baseline has nothing to
tune.

\subsection{Sweeps and Evaluation}

Each disturbance is swept alone on each rung, then all together, from none
to well beyond what real hardware produces, with levels spaced finely near
the realistic range (Fig.~\ref{fig:ranges}). The range comes from published
sensor and radio specifications, published operating limits for wind, or
ArduPilot software-in-the-loop runs. We record the turn rates, actuator
saturation, and message ages actually reached, since a sweep that never
reaches an effect's regime cannot show the effect is unimportant.

Each task's primary metric is chosen in advance: the benchmark's return for
navigation, success for collision avoidance, and velocity alignment for
flocking; other outcomes are reported separately. Each learned configuration
uses ten training seeds, and seeds, not episodes, are the independent units.
Each method's own curve is summarized with interquartile means and stratified
bootstrap intervals \cite{agarwal2021precipice,gorsane2022evaluation}. Tests
cover one comparison chosen in advance per question below, corrected for
multiplicity; everything else is exploratory. Event counts (collisions,
deadlocks, assignment switches) are reported at every level, and replays are
inspected near any change of sign or abrupt fall.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:learning}

The experiments answer five questions. For each, we recorded a prediction
before the main runs, derived from what each method assumes, and we report
results that contradict a prediction as such:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Does a simple classical baseline belong beside learned methods on the
benchmark?
\item Does the navigation ranking survive realistic sensing?
\item Do fixed-wing aircraft reverse the collision-avoidance ranking, and
why?
\item Does lossy, late communication change which method flocks better?
\item How far does training under realistic conditions shift the learned
curves?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Does a classical baseline belong on the benchmark?}
Learned results on the spread task are usually reported against one another,
without a classical reference \cite{papoudakis2021benchmarking}, and a
learned policy has been compared with optimal assignment only in its
authors' own environment, where it came close but did not match it
\cite{muthusamy2024unlabeled}. We compare MAPPO, trained with published
settings, with the assignment baseline at the benchmark's own setting, and
expect the baseline to match or exceed it on coverage and path cost. We then
repeat the comparison with both methods on velocity commands, the learned
one retrained; this links the benchmark's ranking to the vehicle rungs.
\stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{Does the navigation ranking survive realistic sensing?}
Assignment should hold until the noise is comparable to the differences
between agents' distances to their landmarks, then fall sharply as agents
swap targets; MAPPO should degrade gradually. We expect the gap to change
sign within the error of consumer satellite positioning, and count
assignment switches and path cost to explain it. \stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{Do fixed-wing aircraft reverse collision avoidance?}
ORCA assumes an agent can slow down or stop. Aircraft held near constant
speed settle on parallel tracks without resolving the conflict
\cite{durand2018csorca}, and we found no learned versus classical comparison
on fixed-wing aircraft. We expect the gap at zero disturbance to have the
opposite sign on the fixed-wing rungs from the point-agent rung, and the
multirotor, which can nearly stop, to stay close to the point agents. If ORCA
also fails on the kinematic aircraft, the inability to stop is the cause; if
only on the 6-DOF aircraft, the dynamics are. We count deadlocks and closest
approach. \stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{Does lossy communication change who flocks better?}
A learned flocking controller's advantage comes from information passed over
several hops: Tolstaya et al.'s graph network beat a local controller at a
short communication range, where that information matters most
\cite{tolstaya2020gnn}. Lost and late messages degrade it, while the
classical rules use only direct neighbours. We expect the gap to change sign
within the loss and delay of real low-power radios. \stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{How far does realistic training shift the curves?}
Training under realistic or randomized conditions removes the learned
policy's unfamiliarity with the disturbance, while a classical method's
broken assumption remains. We expect it to shift the learned gap curves in
their favour, moving any change of sign toward larger disturbances, without
removing the loss. We compare the three training conditions as groups of ten
seeds. \stub{Results pending.}

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:limits}

\stub{Summarize what the gap curves show across the three tasks and the
vehicle rungs: where the benchmark's ranking held, where it changed, which
factor changed it, and what realistic training recovered.}

Every comparison in this study is between simulations. The ladder measures
whether a simplified benchmark predicts what a checked, more detailed
simulation says, not what real vehicles would do, and a more detailed
simulation is not automatically closer to reality
\cite{truong2022rethinking}. The results are therefore guidance within the
tested models and regimes. Where a model's source was measured on hardware,
as the Crazyflie's parameters were \cite{forster2015crazyflie}, matching that
source carries some of its evidence. The multirotor's motor lag is uncertain
by a factor of two, and spin and propeller wash over a wing are not modeled.

Several directions follow. A few real flights, such as three Crazyflies on
the navigation task, would test the multirotor rung directly, and measured
flight data for a fixed-wing aircraft would do the same for the aircraft
rungs. Gusts, larger teams, and further tasks would extend the sweeps.
More broadly, we hope that reporting a gap curve with its realistic range,
rather than a single ranking, becomes a common way to compare multi-robot
methods, so that a benchmark result says how far it can be trusted.
