\begin{abstract}
Language models are increasingly proposed as high-level managers for robot
fleets. They can respond to events while a dispatcher assigns work and each
vehicle's controller handles flight. But an order that helps one part of a
fleet can change what the rest can do later. We present Ripple, a
multi-agent simulator that runs managers, dispatchers, vehicle autonomies,
controllers, and dynamics through complete missions and scores their
flights. In a regional delivery study, managers can set local rules, lend an
aircraft for one flight, or relocate aircraft and stock. We find that
language-model managers over-help: they relocate vehicles and stock even
when local rules and lent flights produce more on-time deliveries. The cost
is in the action itself: with no model involved, relocating instead of
keeping to local rules loses about one percentage point at four hubs and
ten at 64, and the loss falls at the hubs that gave the
aircraft. We use the model in three roles. It gives the orders itself; it
reads a classical planner's orders and drops the ones it rejects; or it
writes the rules before the day starts and never acts again. In all three it
moves aircraft and stock between hubs, and the loss again falls at the hubs
that gave. Telling it what its past moves cost does not reliably reduce
them. Showing only hubs with problems holds some models back at 16 hubs and
none at 64; a rule that keeps a reserve of vehicles and stock at each hub
blocks excessive moves directly. All results are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robotic systems increasingly divide decisions across levels. A high-level
component chooses a goal or subtask, and a controller or learned policy
turns that choice into motion. Recent robot foundation models use this
structure to carry out longer tasks: $\pi_{0.5}$ predicts semantic subtasks
before low-level actions, and GR00T N1 combines reasoning about a task with
physical control \citep{black2025pi05,nvidia2025gr00t}. A team of robots
adds decisions about who should act, how work should be shared, and what to
do when the situation changes. Language models are attractive at this
level because an operator can describe a new task or disruption in ordinary
language while established software continues to dispatch and control the
robots.

Language-model planning for teams has taken several forms. SMART-LLM
breaks an instruction into tasks for groups of robots
\citep{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments to a mixed team
after feedback \citep{liu2025coherent}. Other systems compare centralized
and local planners \citep{chen2024scalable}, direct large collectives from
language \citep{vatnsdal2026compass}, or dispatch and reposition a fleet
of vehicles \citep{lyu2025llmoddr}. These systems show ways to connect a
model to a team of robots. A fleet that operates through a day poses a further
question: an order can solve the problem in front of the manager and still
leave the fleet less able to handle what happens next.
As the fleet grows, the manager's interface matters too: which parts of
the fleet should the model see, and which changes should it be allowed to
make?

We study these questions with a regional delivery fleet. Each depot, which we
call a hub, has its own aircraft and stock. Requests arrive throughout the
day, and a dispatcher assigns them to available vehicles. When urgent
requests surge across several hubs, a manager can change which requests
take priority, allow an aircraft from a neighboring hub to make a delivery
and return home, or move aircraft and stock to the affected hub. Each
choice may help with the surge. Moving aircraft and stock also leaves the
neighboring hub with less capacity when its next requests arrive. The
manager must decide whether the immediate help is worth that later cost.

Judging a plan only when the surge begins hides this later cost. The
same aircraft may serve another request after returning home, or it may
still be based elsewhere. Its route and return time determine which later
requests it can reach before their deadlines. We therefore compare
managers on the same stream of requests and disruptions, and score what
the vehicles flew and delivered over the complete mission. Two runs with
the same requests can still diverge if a model gives different replies.
Each order changes the fleet state seen at later decisions.

We built Ripple, a multi-agent simulator, to run these comparisons.
A manager gives orders to a dispatcher; vehicle autonomies, controllers,
and motion models carry them out on the same clock. Metrics follow the
flights and requests after each order, including work at hubs outside the
immediate disruption. We can change the manager, its view of the fleet, or
the vehicle model while running matched missions. Model replies are saved
so each observed mission can be inspected and replayed.
The same regional mission can also run 1,024 hubs and 5,120 aircraft. At that
size, a manager's view and the reach of its orders become part of the
planning problem.

We first use Ripple to separate the actions of a structured replanner.
In the regional missions studied here, local rules improve on-time
delivery, and letting aircraft serve neighboring hubs adds to that gain.
Moving aircraft and stock between hubs lowers on-time delivery, with a
larger loss as the region grows from four to 64 hubs, and the loss lands
at the hubs that gave the aircraft. We then put a language model in three
roles: it gives the orders itself, it reviews the replanner's orders and
drops the ones it rejects, or it writes the rules once before the day
starts. In every role it moves aircraft and stock between hubs, and the
loss again lands at the hubs that gave. Telling it what its last move cost
does not reliably reduce the moves. Showing it only the
hubs with problems holds some models back at 16 hubs and none at 64; a
rule that keeps a reserve at each hub limits the moves directly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Planning for robot teams}
Language models have been used to decompose tasks and assign them to teams
of heterogeneous robots. SMART-LLM forms coalitions for subtasks
\citep{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments after feedback
\citep{liu2025coherent}. Chen et al. compare centralized, decentralized,
and hybrid organizations as team size grows \citep{chen2024scalable}.
Scale-Plan filters a larger action graph before calling a model
\citep{gupta2026scaleplan}. COMPASS studies language-conditioned collective
behavior with up to 1,024 robots in short flocking trials
\citep{vatnsdal2026compass}. These systems ask how a team can carry out a
command. We ask what happens after a fleet manager changes the distribution
of vehicles and stock over a day, including at hubs it was not trying to
help.

\paragraph{Managing a fleet}
Dispatch and repositioning are coupled: a vehicle sent to one area is no
longer available in another. LLM-ODDR uses a language model to dispatch
orders and reposition drivers; its surge study uses 300 taxis
\citep{lyu2025llmoddr}. In aerial robotics, COLA executes multi-UAV
missions through collaborating agents \citep{ludwig2026cola}, and
Web-of-Drones connects a language agent to flight tools
\citep{iannoli2026webofdrones}. The delivery problem here has heterogeneous
vehicles, hub stock, deadlines, and rules that change during a mission.
We give the manager several ways to help, including temporary service by a
neighbor, and measure their later cost in the same operating day.

\paragraph{Interfaces and checks}
The interface through which an agent observes and acts can change its
behavior without changing the model. SWE-agent shows this for software
agents \citep{yang2024sweagent}; work on tool documentation and context
selection studies particular parts of that interface
\citep{hsieh2023tooldoc,lodha2026lesscontext}. In robotics, SayCan grounds
skill selection in affordances \citep{ahn2022saycan}, while Safety Chip and
RoboGuard check proposed actions against constraints
\citep{yang2024safetychip,ravichandran2025roboguard}.
LLM-Modulo argues for verifiers around a model rather than treating its
output as an executable plan \citep{kambhampati2024llmmodulo}. Our checker
rejects infeasible or rule-breaking orders; a separate guard limits how
much capacity can leave any one hub. We compare those mechanisms with a
smaller view and with a model that reviews a classical replanner's orders.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:ripple}

Ripple simulates a set of agents on a shared clock. Its design follows
SCRIMMAGE's separation of agents, plugins, and world interactions
\citep{demarco2018}. Each agent owns physical state and a stack of plugins.
The engine advances every agent through the same ordered phases, so a fleet
decision, a vehicle's response, and a later request belong to one mission
history. A research crate supplies the agents and algorithms for a study;
the engine supplies timing, messages, recording, and metrics. The delivery
crate used here defines hubs, a dispatcher, couriers, manager orders, and
the score. The same engine can run a different autonomy or motion model
without changing the simulation loop.

\subsection{Composing a fleet}

An autonomy chooses what an agent does. A controller turns its commands
into motion inputs, and a motion model advances the physical state. Sensors
produce readings; estimators update the belief available to autonomy and
controllers. Interactions, networks, and metrics operate across agents.
The mission selects these components for each agent. In this study,
fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors share the request stream but use
different controllers and motion models. Fixed-wing aircraft make a
parachute drop on a pass; multirotors can also land on hospital rooftops.
A manager need only choose among actions the vehicle autonomies expose.

Ripple leaves the behavior vocabulary to the research crate. Here, the
Courier autonomy handles the vehicle's lifecycle---idle, serving a
request, returning, reloading, relocating, or lost---and runs a delivery
skill for each assignment. The skills are \texttt{drop} and \texttt{land},
with an optional waypoint for a route around a closed area. The dispatcher
checks whether a vehicle is available, has the skill, and has sufficient
estimated endurance for the requested flight. These checks occur before
an assignment; the score is based on what the vehicle subsequently flies.
A request that looks feasible from the manager's summary can still become
late because the aircraft has to reach it and return before taking another.

\subsection{From a manager order to a flight}

The dispatcher assigns individual requests to vehicles. At each decision
it considers idle vehicles at the site's hub, stock, skill, estimated range,
and arrival time. A request it cannot assign is retried. This dispatcher
runs in every condition, including the condition with no manager. The
manager acts above it when an event begins or at a scheduled check-in. It
can change local priorities and restrictions, permit another hub to serve a
site, relocate aircraft, or transfer stock by flight. The manager never
assigns an individual request.

An order changes the dispatcher's future choices as well as the immediate
flight. \texttt{serve\_from} lets a neighboring hub serve a site, after
which the aircraft returns to its own hub. \texttt{reposition} flies an
idle aircraft to a new hub, which becomes its home. \texttt{transfer} uses
a vehicle to carry stock between hubs. The latter two consume aircraft
time and leave the hub that gave them less capacity for later requests.
This difference is central to the experiment: a move can solve the current
shortage and still reduce the day's on-time share. Ripple records each
order's acceptance and the subsequent flight, delivery, and hub state.

The manager can be a scripted policy, a structured replanner, or a language
model. The same orders pass through the same dispatcher and Courier code.
For a model, the delivery crate writes a view of the fleet and active
events, sends it to a model client, parses the reply, and applies the
resulting orders. A model can also review a replanner's proposed orders
and choose which to drop. A policy file offers a third arrangement: the
model writes rules once, and those rules run without a model call during
the mission. These arrangements change the manager, not the vehicles or
scoring.

\subsection{Running matched missions}

Ripple's ordered phases put the manager and vehicles on the same timeline:
autonomy acts, controllers and motion models execute, sensors and world
interactions update, messages are routed, estimators update belief, and
metrics observe the result. The manager's view is formed from reported
fleet state and events rather than the physical truth used by the metric.
A closure, for example, is judged from a vehicle's actual position during
the closure window. A delivery whose flight broke an active rule does not
count as served, even if the manager ordered it in time.

Requests and events are drawn from random numbers keyed by hub and event
index. A seed therefore defines a common day for managers that give
different orders. Randomness in the simulator is keyed by purpose and
stable identities, so a run repeats exactly. We also save each model reply
with the prompt that produced it. An unchanged model run can then be
replayed without another call. These properties make it possible to
inspect a failed mission and to compare interventions on matched request
streams. A changed view or policy changes future prompts and may require
new model replies; saved replies
alone do not make different conditions identical.

The simulator carries the whole region between manager calls. At 1,024
hubs, it advances 5,120 aircraft through 306,000 steps of 0.1 seconds.
Requests arrive for the first eight hours of that 8.5-hour day. An initial
release timing check without model calls completed an 8.5-hour day with
1,280 aircraft on one worker in about 16 minutes. We measure simulator
runtime separately from model inference.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We use Ripple to study fleet decisions on a day of regional medical
delivery. Hubs lie on a square grid 15~km apart
(Figure~\ref{fig:region}). Each has three fixed-wing
aircraft, two multirotors, four blood-product stocks, six clinics, and two
hospital
rooftops with nearby ground pads. Background delivery requests arrive at
a rate of twelve per hub per hour, with additional requests generated by
surge events. Routine, urgent, and trauma requests have two-hour, 30-minute,
and 15-minute deadlines. The reported experiments use four-hour request
streams at 4, 16, and 64 hubs, or 20 to 320 aircraft, and allow 30 minutes
for the last requests to finish.

The scale is that of a real operation. Zipline's long-range aircraft
cruises at 97~km/h and flies round trips of over 190~km
\citep{zipline2023factsheet}; our fixed-wing aircraft cruise at 90~km/h,
and the 15~km hub spacing and 4 to 13~km site distances sit well inside
that range.

Events include trauma surges, stock shortages, hub outages, closed airspace,
weather restrictions, priority changes, and rooftop closures. Their counts
are drawn per hub, so a larger region encounters more disruptions. The
request and event stream is matched by seed across managers. The base
fleet uses a kinematic fixed-wing aircraft and a point multirotor with
limited speed and endurance. Ripple also has checked six-degree-of-freedom
fixed-wing and multirotor models; the results here do not establish that
the manager ranking persists under those models.

The outcome is the on-time share of requests, weighted one for routine,
two for urgent, and four for trauma. We also report late and missed
requests, lost vehicles, rule breaches, accepted and refused orders, moves
of aircraft or stock, and temporary cross-hub service permissions. A
\emph{move} is an applied reposition or stock transfer; a \emph{lend} is
an applied \texttt{serve\_from} permission, not necessarily a completed
flight. That distinction matters when interpreting order counts. The
score is computed from deliveries and trajectories, not from the manager's
intent.

We compare six classical policies before interpreting model performance.
\emph{None} is the dispatcher alone. \emph{Local} adds rules for closures,
grounding, priority, and rooftop delivery without moving capacity between
hubs. \emph{Serve} adds temporary permissions for neighboring hubs to serve
sites. \emph{Move} adds aircraft relocations and stock transfers to local
rules, without the lending orders. The \emph{replanner} combines these
actions and tops up low stock at check-ins. A \emph{return} variant sends
relocated aircraft home after an event ends. The policies use the same
order interface and are compared on the same day.

Model managers use one frozen system prompt and one text for each event
kind; the prompt, the event texts, one complete call and reply, and the
per-run counts of calls, orders, refusals, and unreadable replies are in
the appendix, which is in the
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/EthanMBoos/ripple/tree/main/paper/appendix}}
We vary the view and feedback supplied by the delivery crate: hub
summaries, summaries with order outcomes, a feasibility checker, a line
reporting the lending hub's state after a move, a guard on the lending hub's
reserve, and a view that shows only exceptions. We also test a model as a
veto over the replanner and as the author of a policy file. Four models,
Claude Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus and GPT-6 Luna, are compared in two roles, as
live managers shown only the exceptions and as vetoes over the replanner, on
three seeds at 16 hubs and two at 64. The step-by-step comparison of views
and feedback and the policy-writing comparison use Haiku and Sonnet on two
and three seeds at 16 hubs. The classical grid and jittered comparisons
use the same ten seeds at each hub size. Each table states its sample.

The subsections ask, in order: what a replanner's help is worth and which
part of it; whether any interface gets a model to stop relocating; what a
model drops when given a planner's list; whether a model that writes the
rules converges on the right ones; how spare capacity changes the cost of
moving; whether the result depends on the model; and what a model does on
a day with little to manage. Section~\ref{sec:api-scale} then asks which
view and order set remain useful as the region grows.

measured against local rules. At four hubs, return and lending nearly tie

The main finding persists off the lattice. With every hub displaced by
up to 3~km, no two closer than 10~km, and one displaced layout per seed
shared by all policies, lending has the highest mean at all three sizes.
Relocating costs 0.83, 5.31, and 8.78 points against local rules at 4, 16,
and 64 hubs (the jittered rows of Table~\ref{tab:decomposition}).

The decomposition separates the immediate event response from its effect on
later work. A lent flight comes home. A permanent
reposition does not, and a stock transfer occupies another aircraft.
Figure~\ref{fig:region} shows the mechanism on one surge: the surge area
is served about equally well either way, and the whole difference is the
lending hub's own requests after its aircraft left. Figure~\ref{fig:loss}
shows the same pattern across the full simulated days. Across ten 16-hub days,
relocating lost 68.5 more requests a day than local rules, counting late
and undelivered together: 77.2 more at the hubs that gave aircraft or
stock, 8.9 fewer at the hubs that received them, and 0.2 more elsewhere.
Per hundred of their own requests, the hubs that gave lost about 17 more,
the hubs that received 3 fewer, and the rest almost none. Across ten
64-hub days, the hubs that gave lost 445.5 more requests a day than under
local rules, about 17 per hundred of their own, while the hubs that
received lost 3 fewer per hundred and the rest almost none.
The finding is specific to these missions and
policies: a different relocation rule, demand distribution, or return
policy could change the comparison.

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} compares two models in the live manager role. With
summaries alone, both make dozens of vehicle and stock moves on each seed

information in another form.

Two interfaces change the intervention more directly. A guard refuses
moves that would leave the hub giving them below a capacity threshold, reducing
Haiku's moves from 38 and 22 with feedback to 12 and 5. The replanner
with the same guard scores 0.72 and 0.75 and Haiku with it 0.70 and 0.75,
so the guard does the work and the model adds nothing over it. The exceptions
view leaves Haiku with 3 and 1 moves, close to the local policy's score;
Sonnet still makes 29 and 14 moves under the same view. The difference
between the two models is as useful as the aggregate score: withholding
routine fleet state can reduce unnecessary orders, but its effect is not
a property of the view alone. At 64 hubs the exceptions view holds no
model back (Section~\ref{sec:models}): a region that size has dozens of
hubs with a problem at every call, so the view no longer hides much, and
every model relocates.

\subsection{What does a model drop when given a planner's list?}
\label{sec:veto}

We next put the model after the replanner. The replanner proposes orders;
the model can drop any of them, but cannot add its own. On the two
16-hub seeds, Haiku retains 9 and 15 of the replanner's 12 and 15 moves,
while retaining 72 and 80 of its 144 and 120 lending permissions. Sonnet
retains 14 moves on each seed and 48 and 80 lending permissions. The veto
improves the score over the replanner in most runs, yet it does not recover
the local policy's advantage. Given a proposal list, these models remove
many temporary permissions and retain most permanent moves. The counts
refer to permissions and orders, not completed borrowed flights. The
pattern holds on a third 16-hub seed and on two seeds at 64 hubs
(Section~\ref{sec:models}): in 10 of those 12 runs the model keeps a
larger share of the replanner's moves than of its lending permissions,
and every veto run scores under local rules, by 0.01 to 0.07.

\subsection{Does a model converge on the right policy given time?}
\label{sec:revision}

A model can also write the rules that run the mission. We give Haiku and
Sonnet the mission description and a small policy language, then execute
each written policy without further model calls. Both initial policies
parse and run. Both order more stock transfers than the
replanner, including transfers from two hubs on a surge. We then show
each model the aggregate score, late deliveries, moves, and lending counts
from a day at 16 and 64 hubs and ask it to revise once.

Haiku keeps its moves but makes repositions smaller and gives them a return
time. Sonnet reduces its move count and adds trauma priority to its rules.
The revisions improve their first policies by 0.01 to 0.05 in the tested
runs, yet their scores across three seeds at 16 hubs remain 0.66, 0.71, and
0.73 for Haiku and 0.66, 0.71, and 0.72 for Sonnet, below the local
policy's 0.74, 0.79, and 0.76. In this policy-writing role, extra time and
aggregate outcome feedback improve the rules without discovering the
simple alternative of leaving capacity at its home hub and lending service
when required.

The four models respond differently to the same interface
(Table~\ref{tab:models}), and the region's size changes the answer. At 16

points under local rules on every seed, never above. The live runs also
lose vehicles, which happens when an aircraft runs out of endurance away

appendix gives the counts for every run.

The exception is Opus under the exceptions view at 16 hubs on the third
seed: 0.80 against 0.76, with 58 moves. On that day its relocations
landed where the next requests arrived: 14 requests were missed for want
of an aircraft against 40 under local rules and 28 under lending. The same
arm scored 0.67 and 0.68 on the other 16-hub seeds and 0.63 and 0.54 at

relocating happened to pay, not a model that learned when to relocate.
Restraint does not come with model size, and no interface is best for
every model.

Do the models lose because they move? The classical runs say a move costs
the hub that gave the aircraft. If the models' moves are what costs them,
their losses should sit at the same hubs. To check, we replayed all 40
model runs from their saved replies, this time counting late and
undelivered requests at each hub, and compared each hub with local rules
on the same day (Figure~\ref{fig:loss}). We sort the hubs in each run into
three groups: hubs that
gave aircraft or stock, hubs that only received, and hubs that did neither.
For every 100 of its own requests, a hub that gave lost 11 more than under
local rules. A hub that only received lost none. A hub that did neither
lost 3. Of everything the models lost against local rules, 90 percent was
at the hubs that gave. This holds for Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus at both
sizes and in both roles, where the share is 80 percent or more. GPT-6 Luna
is different. It rarely moves, so only a third of its loss at 16 hubs is at
hubs that gave. Its loss comes from lending: on one 64-hub day it made five
moves, granted 908 lending permissions, and lost 331 requests at hubs that
neither gave nor received. On the one day a model beat local rules, Opus
moved among 14 hubs and those hubs lost nothing. So the models lose because
they move, and the hub that was asked to help pays.

\subsection{What does a model do on a quiet day?}
\label{sec:quiet}

We reduce event density to one quarter of the main mission's rate.
At 16 hubs, the dispatcher alone scores 0.90 and 0.88 on the two seeds,
and local rules score 0.91 on both. Haiku and Sonnet with outcome feedback
give between 19 and 62 orders where local rules give five or six, including
7 to 34 moves. They score 0.89 and 0.88, and 0.89 and 0.87. Even on a
quieter day, almost every scheduled call sees some exception somewhere in
the region. The result is thus a tendency to intervene on small but real
problems, rather than evidence that models invent work from an entirely
empty view.

\section{The Fleet API at Scale}
\label{sec:api-scale}

The fleet API has two parts: what the model sees and what it can order. Its
report can describe every hub or show only hubs with problems. Its orders
can change local priorities, lend an aircraft for one flight, or move
aircraft and stock between hubs. Together, the report and the orders set
the size of the model's task and how far its decisions can reach.

At 16 hubs, the exceptions report keeps Haiku and GPT-6 Luna to at most
four moves on each tested day. At 64 hubs, both make more moves, and all
four models move capacity under the same report (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
As the region grows, even a report that omits healthy hubs can contain many
unrelated problems. A model managing them in one call must decide where
help is needed and whether the hubs asked to give can spare an aircraft
for their own later requests.

\emph{Planned test.} At 256 and 1,024 hubs (1,280 and 5,120 aircraft), each
hub keeps the same fleet, request rate, and event process. Classical policies
on matched days establish what moving costs at each size. Each model manager
receives one 16-hub cluster and may set local rules and lend flights. A
manager above the clusters sees one summary per cluster and may move
aircraft and stock between them. These runs test whether a bounded local
view supports useful decisions and whether region-wide moves bring back
the losses at giving hubs. Live orders, vetoes over a replanner, and rules
written before the day begins test whether the model should give orders,
screen them, or set the rules once. We measure on-time score, losses at
giving hubs, moves per 100 hubs, calls, input tokens, and simulation time.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The classical decomposition gives the model results a physical explanation.
A manager can help a stressed hub by changing priority or allowing
neighboring aircraft to serve its sites. Those actions expire or end with
the flight. Relocating vehicles changes the giving hub's future capacity, and
stock transfers use aircraft that could have delivered requests. In this
mission, the latter actions often cost more than they gain. The same
pattern appears in a structured replanner and in several model roles, so
it cannot be reduced to a single model making a malformed call.

The interfaces change different parts of this behavior. Outcome feedback and
a line showing the lending hub's state provide information, but they do not
prevent moves. The exceptions view limits what the manager sees and holds
some models back at 16 hubs; at 64 hubs the exceptions themselves are
plentiful and every model relocates. A guard on each hub's reserve
makes the constraint explicit and refuses orders that cross it. A model veto
can remove bad orders from a planner, but these models also remove lending
permissions while keeping many relocations. A useful next design would let
the model propose an intervention and let a planner price its opportunity
cost over the remaining day before committing it. Ripple can compare that
design with the present ones on the same missions.

The study has clear limits. Its request distribution, event rates, hub
geometry, and four-hour horizon are simulated. The reported fleets use
kinematic vehicle models, so the result concerns fleet allocation under
these flight times and ranges; it does not establish a difference caused by
six-degree-of-freedom dynamics. The model comparisons have three seeds at 16
hubs and two at 64. The four models were reached through two command-line
tools that wrap them differently, one keeping a coding agent's tools and one
not; calling the model APIs directly would make the cleaner cross-model
comparison. One model run in 40 beat local rules, so the claim is about the
average and the spread of a model's days, not a bound. Longer days,
different demand patterns, and policies that return vehicles sooner would
test where relocating begins to pay.

Ripple's broader role is to make these questions experimental. The manager
can be changed without rewriting the courier, the vehicle model without
rewriting the manager, and the score can follow the consequences of each
choice through a complete day. The important outcome here is not a
universal instruction to avoid relocation. It is evidence that a fleet
manager's apparently helpful action needs to be judged by the work it
displaces later and elsewhere.
