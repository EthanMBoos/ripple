\begin{abstract}
Language models are increasingly proposed as managers for robot fleets,
issuing orders that vehicles execute. An order may address an urgent
request while leaving the fleet less able to serve later ones. We present
Ripple, a multi-agent simulator for studying fleet managers over complete
missions. Ripple lets us vary managers, dispatchers, vehicle behaviors,
controllers, and motion models on matched missions. Without model calls,
one worker runs an 8.5-hour day with 1,280 aircraft in 16 minutes and
with 5,120 in about two and a half hours. In a regional delivery study,
managers set local rules, allow a neighboring hub to serve requests, or
move aircraft and stock. Language-model managers over-help: they
relocate aircraft and stock when local rules and lending serve more
requests on time. At 64 hubs, even a fixed relocation policy scores
13 points below local rules, with the added misses mainly at hubs
that gave. Relocation helps during some longer surges. When given the
surge end and whole-day objective, Opus reaches a fixed return rule's
score at two hours, but orders five times as many moves. We also test a
fleet API for choosing which hub should lend aircraft. That choice
changes deliveries, but a simple rule based on spare aircraft matches
Haiku and Opus at 16, 64, and 256 hubs without model calls. All results
are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robot systems increasingly divide a longer task into what to do next
and how to execute it. $\pi_{0.5}$ predicts a semantic subtask, such as
picking up a plate, before generating actions with the same model
\cite{black2025pi05}. GR00T 2 lets a separate reasoning agent choose
steps and monitor a robot policy as it carries them out
\cite{nvidia2026gr00t2}. These systems now handle long, multi-stage
tasks on individual robots. A fleet planner must also decide which
robot should act, how work should be shared, and what help now costs
later. Language models are attractive for this role because an operator
can describe new tasks or disruptions in ordinary language. Their orders
pass through dispatchers, vehicle autonomy, and controllers; the
effects unfold across the whole fleet.

Language-model planning for teams has taken several forms. SMART-LLM
breaks an instruction into tasks for groups of robots
\cite{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments to a mixed team
after feedback \cite{liu2025coherent}. Other systems compare centralized
and local planners \cite{chen2024scalable}, direct large collectives from
language \cite{vatnsdal2026compass}, or dispatch and reposition a fleet
of vehicles \cite{lyu2025llmoddr}. These systems show ways to connect a
model to a team of robots. We study two questions about a language model
managing a fleet through a full day. First, when requests and disruptions
compete for the same aircraft and stock, how does the manager respond, and
where do its decisions make the fleet worse off? Second, what does a
manager need to see to make a fleet decision, and does a model improve
on a simple rule as the fleet grows? This second question concerns the
fleet API: the report given to the model and the changes it may order.

We study these questions with a regional delivery fleet. Each depot, which we
call a hub, has its own aircraft and stock. Requests arrive throughout the
day, and a dispatcher assigns them to available vehicles. When urgent
requests surge across several hubs, a manager can change which requests
take priority, allow an aircraft from a neighboring hub to make a delivery
and return home, or move aircraft and stock to the affected hub. Each
choice may help with the surge. Moving aircraft and stock also leaves the
neighboring hub with less capacity when its next requests arrive. The
manager must decide whether the immediate help is worth that later cost.

Judging a plan only when the surge begins hides this later cost. The
same aircraft may serve another request after returning home, or it may
still be based elsewhere. Its route and return time determine which later
requests it can reach before their deadlines. We therefore compare
managers on the same stream of requests and disruptions, and score what
the vehicles flew and delivered over the complete mission. Two runs with
the same requests can still diverge if a model gives different replies.
Each order changes the fleet state seen at later decisions.

We built Ripple, a multi-agent simulator, to run these comparisons.
A manager gives orders to a dispatcher; vehicle autonomies, controllers,
and motion models carry them out on the same clock. Metrics follow the
flights and requests after each order, including work outside the immediate
disruption. We can change the manager, its view of the fleet, or the vehicle
model while running matched missions. Model replies are saved so each
observed mission can be inspected and replayed. The fixed-policy study runs
the same 8.5-hour mission with up to 5,120 aircraft. That scale lets us
measure how a change to one hub affects work elsewhere while requests keep
arriving across the region.
Our contribution is an experimental way to connect a manager's orders to
their later effects on other robots, and to compare a language model with
rules given the same information and authority.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Planning for robot teams}
Language models have been used to decompose tasks and assign them to teams
of heterogeneous robots. SMART-LLM forms coalitions for subtasks
\cite{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments after feedback
\cite{liu2025coherent}. Chen et al. compare centralized, decentralized,
and hybrid organizations as team size grows \cite{chen2024scalable}.
Scale-Plan filters a larger action graph before calling a model
\cite{gupta2026scaleplan}. COMPASS studies language-conditioned collective
behavior with up to 1,024 robots in short flocking trials
\cite{vatnsdal2026compass}. These systems ask how a team can carry out a
command. We ask what happens after a fleet manager changes the distribution
of vehicles and stock over a day, including at hubs it was not trying to
help.

\paragraph{Managing a fleet}
Dispatch and repositioning are coupled: a vehicle sent to one area is no
longer available in another. Work on robotic mobility-on-demand has
modeled this as a rebalancing problem, minimizing empty moves while
maintaining service across stations \cite{pavone2012load}, or jointly
scheduling service and rebalancing with predictive control
\cite{zhang2016mpc}. LLM-ODDR uses a language model to dispatch
orders and reposition drivers; its surge study uses 300 taxis
\cite{lyu2025llmoddr}. In aerial robotics, COLA executes multi-UAV
missions through collaborating agents \cite{ludwig2026cola}, and
Web-of-Drones connects a language agent to flight tools
\cite{iannoli2026webofdrones}. The delivery problem here has heterogeneous
vehicles, hub stock, deadlines, and rules that change during a mission.
We give the manager several ways to help, including temporary service by a
neighbor, and measure their later cost in the same operating day. Fixed
policies run with up to 5,120 aircraft. Live regional managers are tested
with 80 and 320 aircraft; fleet API models also choose lenders for a
region with 1,280 aircraft.

\paragraph{Interfaces and checks}
The interface through which an agent observes and acts can change its
behavior without changing the model. SWE-agent shows this for software
agents \cite{yang2024sweagent}; work on tool documentation and context
selection studies particular parts of that interface
\cite{hsieh2023tooldoc,lodha2026lesscontext}. In robotics, SayCan grounds
skill selection in affordances \cite{ahn2022saycan}, while Safety Chip and
RoboGuard check proposed actions against constraints
\cite{yang2024safetychip,ravichandran2025roboguard}.
LLM-Modulo argues for verifiers around a model rather than treating its
output as an executable plan \cite{kambhampati2024llmmodulo}. Our checker
rejects infeasible or rule-breaking orders; a separate guard limits how
much capacity can leave any one hub. We compare those mechanisms with a
smaller view and with a model that reviews a replanner's orders.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:ripple}

Ripple runs a fleet whose manager and vehicles can be changed independently.
Its design follows SCRIMMAGE's separation of agents, plugins, and world
interactions \cite{demarco2018}. Each agent owns physical state and a set of
plugins. The engine supplies one clock, ordered phases, messages, recording,
and metrics. The delivery crate supplies hubs, a dispatcher, couriers,
manager orders, and a score. We can change a manager or vehicle model while
keeping the rest of the mission fixed.

\subsection{Composing a fleet}

An autonomy chooses what an agent does. A controller turns its commands
into motion inputs, and a motion model advances its physical state. Sensors
produce readings; estimators update the belief used by autonomy and
controllers. Interactions, networks, and metrics operate across agents.
The mission selects these components. Here fixed-wing aircraft and
multirotors share the request stream but use different controllers and
motion models. Fixed-wing aircraft drop by parachute; multirotors can also
land on hospital rooftops.

The delivery crate's Courier autonomy handles assignments, returns,
reloading, and relocation. Each assignment runs a delivery skill. The
skill library defines which aircraft can \texttt{drop} or \texttt{land}
and how they behave at a site; a route may also pass around closed airspace.
The dispatcher checks stock, aircraft availability, skill, estimated range,
and arrival time before assigning a request. A manager can change the rules,
but the dispatcher chooses the skill and the courier flies it. The score
follows the completed flight.

\subsection{From a manager order to a flight}

The same dispatcher assigns requests under every policy, including no
manager. A manager acts when an event begins or at a check-in. It can
change local priorities and restrictions, permit another hub to serve a
site, relocate aircraft, or transfer stock. It does not assign flights.

The orders change what the dispatcher can do next. \texttt{serve\_from}
lets a neighbor serve a site and return home. \texttt{reposition} flies
an idle aircraft to a new home hub. \texttt{transfer} carries stock between
hubs. The latter two use a flight and leave the giving hub with less for
later requests. Ripple records which orders were accepted and the flights,
deliveries, and hub states that follow.

Fixed rules and models use this same order interface. A model may issue
orders during the day, reject a replanner's proposals, or write the rules
before the day. The dispatcher, couriers, and score remain the same.

\subsection{Running matched missions}

In each step, autonomy acts, controllers and motion models execute,
sensors and interactions update, messages are routed, estimators update
belief, and metrics score the result. The manager sees reported fleet
state and events; the metric uses physical truth. A flight through closed
airspace, for example, does not count as served even if the order was
issued on time.

Requests and events use random numbers keyed by purpose, hub, and event.
The same seed therefore gives different managers the same day. Ripple
saves each model prompt and reply for replay. Changing a view or policy
can change later prompts, so a comparison may need new model calls.

An 8.5-hour mission advances 306,000 steps at 0.1 seconds each. Without
model calls, one worker ran it with 1,280 aircraft in 16 minutes and with
5,120 aircraft in about two and a half hours, using at most 1.5 GB of
memory. Model inference is timed separately.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We first use Ripple to study fleet decisions on a day of regional medical
delivery. Hubs lie on a square grid 15~km apart (Figure~\ref{fig:region}).
Each hub has three fixed-wing aircraft, two multirotors, four blood-product
stocks, six clinics, and two hospitals with rooftops and nearby ground
pads. Starting with the same aircraft and stock at each hub makes the
cost to a giving hub easier to see. Delivery requests arrive at twelve
per hub per hour, with more during
surges. Routine, urgent, and trauma requests have two-hour, 30-minute, and
15-minute deadlines. Requests arrive for eight hours, and the fleet has
30 minutes to finish the last of them. We compare fixed policies at 4,
16, and 64 hubs, or 20 to 320 aircraft, and also run them at 256 and
1,024 hubs. The model comparisons for this day use 16 and 64 hubs.
The fleet API study uses a separate mission described in
Section~\ref{sec:scale}. Within each comparison, policies and models
face matched requests and events; the robustness checks deliberately
change the workload or fleet.

The scale is that of a real operation. Zipline's long-range aircraft
cruises at 97~km/h and flies round trips of over 190~km
\cite{zipline2023factsheet}; our fixed-wing aircraft cruise at 90~km/h,
and the 15~km hub spacing and 4 to 13~km site distances sit well inside
that range.

Events include trauma surges, stock shortages, hub outages, closed airspace,
weather restrictions, priority changes, and rooftop closures. Their counts
are drawn per hub, so a larger region encounters more disruptions. The same
seed gives the same requests and events to every manager, so each
comparison is on matched days. The base fleet uses a kinematic fixed-wing
aircraft and a point multirotor with limited speed and endurance. Ripple
also has checked six-degree-of-freedom fixed-wing and multirotor models;
the results here do not establish that the manager ranking persists under
those models.

The outcome is the weighted on-time share $\sum_i w_i s_i/\sum_i w_i$,
where $s_i=1$ if request $i$ arrived by its deadline and zero otherwise.
Weights are one for routine, two for urgent, and four for trauma requests.
We also report late and missed
requests, lost vehicles, rule breaches, accepted and refused orders, moves
of aircraft or stock, and lending permissions. A \emph{move} is an applied
relocation or stock transfer; a \emph{lend} is an applied permission for a
neighboring hub to serve a site, not necessarily a completed flight. The
score is computed from deliveries and trajectories, not from the manager's
intent.

Six fixed policies, rules written once and applied the same way all day,
set the bar. \emph{No manager} is the dispatcher alone. \emph{Local rules}
adds orders that change only what a hub's own aircraft do: priorities,
closures, grounding, and ground-pad delivery. \emph{Lending} adds
permission for a neighboring hub to serve a site, after which the aircraft
comes home. \emph{Relocating} moves up to four fixed-wing aircraft and
two multirotors per surge hub and transfers stock, without lending. It
acts at announcement, before surge requests arrive over the next fifteen
minutes. The \emph{replanner} uses every order and
also tops up low stock at check-ins. \emph{Return} is the replanner with
its relocated aircraft sent home once the event ends.

In the main comparisons, the dispatcher checks each delivery flight but
does not veto a manager's relocation for range or for leaving a hub short.
This lets us measure the consequences of those orders. We test an order
checker and a reserve rule separately in Section~\ref{sec:holds}.

A language model takes three roles. As the \emph{live manager} it is
called when an event begins and every 30 minutes, reads a view of the
fleet, and gives orders. As a \emph{veto} it reads the replanner's proposed
orders and drops the ones it rejects, but adds none. As the \emph{author of
the rules} it writes a policy file once, before the day, which then runs
with no further calls. Four models, Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5,
Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna, are compared in the first two roles, on
three seeds at 16 hubs
and two at 64. Luna uses the OpenAI API; the Claude models use the Claude
command-line client with tools disabled. Both paths receive the same study
system prompt, with no tools available. We leave sampling and reasoning
settings at the clients' defaults and retain one reply per call; repeated
calls on the same state were not tested. The comparisons of what the model
sees and the policy writing use Haiku and Sonnet. These main-study calls
used one frozen system prompt and one text per event kind. The prompt
tells the model to keep trauma and urgent requests on time, and describes
the available orders and the current fleet. It does not give the whole-day
weighted score, say when a surge will end, or quantify the future cost
of moving aircraft or stock. Ripple saves prompts, replies, and per-run
results so a run can be inspected and replayed.

We ask how a language-model manager handles competing needs across a fleet
and where its choices fail. To understand those choices, we first measure
the cost of relocating aircraft and stock under fixed policies, with no
model involved. We then test models as live managers, vetoes, and authors
of rules: what they order, where the resulting missed requests fall, and
what limits harmful moves. Finally, we examine what a model should see and
be allowed to order as the fleet grows. We compare models with fixed
rules for choosing a lender and change the information in their reports.

Table~\ref{tab:policies} compares the fixed policies, with no model involved.
The score is the weighted share of requests delivered on time.
At four hubs, lending scores 0.81 and the replanner
ties local rules at 0.78. At 16 hubs, local rules and lending score 0.74,
the replanner 0.69, and relocating 0.66. The gap between local rules and
relocating reaches 13 points at 64 hubs and stays there at 256 and 1,024.
From 64 hubs on, the replanner does worse than no manager. It grants the
same lending permissions as the lending policy, but also relocates aircraft
and transfers stock. Sending relocated aircraft home restores about half
its gap to local rules at those sizes. Across all 20 days at 16 and 64
hubs, local rules beat both the replanner and relocating.

The result survives two changes to the setup. Displacing hubs by up to
3~km off the grid changes each policy's mean by less than two points.
Increasing the fleet from five to twelve aircraft per hub narrows the
replanner's deficit to about two points at 16 and 64 hubs, without
reversing the ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:region} shows the mechanism on one surge: the surge is
served about equally well, but a hub that gave aircraft later misses its
own requests. Figure~\ref{fig:loss} shows that pattern over whole days.
Under the relocating policy at 16 hubs, giving hubs have 154 more late or
undelivered requests per day than under local rules; receiving hubs recover
seven, leaving 147 more across the region. At 64 hubs, giving hubs lose
16 additional requests per hundred of their own, while receiving hubs
serve two more per hundred.

Can a longer surge repay the move? On otherwise quiet 16-hub days, we
held one five-hub surge for 15, 60, 120, or 240 minutes at the same
request rate, using five matched seeds at each length. At 15 minutes,
relocating scores 0.87 against local rules' 0.94; lending and return
both score about 0.96. At 60 minutes, lending, the replanner, and Return
beat local rules on all five seeds; Return scores 0.73 and the replanner
0.69 against local rules' 0.65, while relocating without lending still
trails at 0.63. At 120 minutes all four alternatives beat local rules on
all five seeds; at 240, none has a higher mean. Moving can pay in the one-
and two-hour surges on this quiet day, especially when aircraft return.
The main comparison has 15-minute surges, where the move costs more than
it gains.

\subsection{What stops a model from relocating}
\label{sec:holds}

We tested Haiku and Sonnet as live managers on two matched 16-hub days,
changing the view and the checks one at a time. With a summary of every
hub, Haiku makes 44 to 52 moves and Sonnet 37 to 66. Reporting the
outcome of the last orders reduces Haiku's moves on one seed, but Sonnet
moves more on both. A feasibility checker and a line showing what the
giving hub kept do not consistently reduce moves either. Information
about past costs does not by itself stop the next move.

A reserve rule does. It refuses orders that would leave a hub with too
few aircraft or too little stock. With this guard, Haiku makes 10 to 25
moves and Sonnet 28 to 38; 29 to 38 orders are refused per day. The
same guard improves the model-free replanner on nine of ten 16-hub days,
tying once, and on all ten 64-hub days. Its mean rises from 0.69 to 0.72
and from 0.62 to 0.69, respectively, without losing aircraft. It still
trails local rules' 0.74 and 0.73; at 64 it trails on all ten days.
Neither guarded model in the two-day interface test consistently beats
local rules.

Showing only hubs with problems reduces Haiku to seven moves on each
16-hub day without improving its score; Sonnet still makes 36 and 48.
This smaller view is the setting of Figure~\ref{fig:models}. At 64 hubs,
all tested models still relocate; there are dozens of problem hubs at
each call. A smaller view can change behavior; it does not enforce a
reserve.

\section{The Fleet API at Scale}
\label{sec:scale}

In Ripple, a dispatcher assigns requests to aircraft and checks which
fleet orders can be carried out. A fleet manager makes the higher-level
choices. The manager can be a fixed rule or a language model. The fleet
API connects them: it is the report sent to the manager and the orders
the dispatcher will accept. We can change either part and compare the
flights that follow on the same mission.

Here the manager has one job. When a hub faces a surge, the dispatcher
lists other hubs that can lend aircraft while keeping half of each
aircraft class at home. For each hub asking for help, the manager picks
one lender from the list or declines help. A selected lender may serve
that hub until the surge ends; its aircraft return home after each
flight. The dispatcher assigns the individual requests under fixed
local rules. This manager cannot relocate aircraft or transfer stock.

We compare two reports for that same decision. The compact report
names each asking hub and up to five possible lenders. For each lender
it shows distance, aircraft available by type, how many of the asking
hub's sites they can reach, the lender's own waiting requests, and time
to the first site. The full report adds the status of every hub in the
region. We also remove parts of the offer lines one at a time to see
which information changes on-time delivery. The allowed help order
stays the same in every comparison.

For this API study, we use a separate mission based on a 7~km hub grid and
unequal aircraft fleets. Overlapping trauma surges lasting an hour can make
the nearest lender different from the one with the most aircraft to
spare. At 64 and 256 hubs, we repeat the 16-hub pattern in four and
sixteen rotated or mirrored blocks. These larger missions test more
simultaneous lending choices of the same kind. We compare local rules
with three ways to choose from the same offers. Random chooses any
listed lender; nearest chooses the closest; spare chooses the lender
with the most available aircraft after subtracting its own waiting
requests. The score is the weighted share of requests delivered before
their deadlines.

aircraft counts, types, and reach, so these runs do not show which part

The models can read the report and choose good lenders across the
region. The score tie does not mean they copied the rule: on newly
affected hubs with several offers, their picks differ from the rule's
between a quarter and half the time at 16 hubs, about a third at 64,
and about two in five at 256. A rule that counts spare aircraft makes
equally good choices at every tested size. For this fleet decision,
model calls do not improve on-time delivery.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

We described Ripple, a simulator for studying fleet decisions over
complete missions. Managers, dispatchers, vehicle behaviors, and motion
models can be varied independently on matched days of requests and
disruptions. One worker simulated an 8.5-hour day with 5,120 aircraft
about 3.4 times faster than real time, without model calls. In the
delivery study, language-model managers over-helped, leaving other hubs
short later. In the fleet API study, models matched a simple lending
rule without improving on it. Ripple shows the effect of each decision
on the work completed across the fleet. Those results raise a question
about the fleet's design: which decisions should reach the language
model?

At the robot level, $\pi_{0.5}$ and GR00T 2 separate choosing a task
from carrying it out; GR00T 2 calls the reasoning and action layers
System 2 and System 1 \cite{black2025pi05,nvidia2026gr00t2}. A fleet
must make that division across many robots.
Flight checks, reserve limits, dispatch, and comparisons between known
offers have clear inputs and objectives. Software can make those
decisions quickly and consistently. The case for a language model begins
when the fleet must interpret a new instruction, resolve competing
priorities, or decide when its usual rules no longer fit the task.
Giving a model every decision because it sits above the robots is not
enough. In our studies, that freedom led to costly help, while a
narrower choice gave the model nothing a simple rule could not do.

Ripple can be used to find this boundary. For each proposed model
decision, give a rule or planner the same information and authority,
then compare completed work over a whole mission. When a rule matches
the model, leave that decision with the rule for the task at hand. When
a model handles changing goals better, give it that responsibility
while the dispatcher keeps flight checks and reserves. The boundary
may shift with the mission: relocation helped under longer surges in
our study, even though it hurt under the main workload.

These experiments use simulated demand, grid hubs, and kinematic
aircraft. The model comparisons cover a small number of days, and the
lending study gives the manager a choice with explicit numerical facts.

Future studies should give the manager a different job: interpret new
goals, resolve changing priorities, or coordinate robots with
different skills. They can also add intermediate managers between a
language model and the vehicles, or give a larger model more time to
reason. The aim is to learn which decisions benefit from that reasoning
and which should stay with rules, planners, and local robot policies.
