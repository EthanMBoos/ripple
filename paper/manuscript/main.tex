\begin{abstract}
Language models are increasingly proposed as high-level managers for robot
fleets. An order that helps one part of a fleet can change what the rest can
do later. We present Ripple, a
multi-agent simulator that runs managers, dispatchers, vehicle autonomies,
controllers, and dynamics through complete missions and scores their
flights. One worker runs an 8.5-hour mission with 1,280 aircraft in about
16 minutes, without model calls; the fixed-policy study reaches 5,120
aircraft. In a regional delivery study, managers set local rules, lend an
aircraft for one flight, or move aircraft and stock. We find that
language-model managers over-help: they relocate vehicles and stock even
when local rules and lent flights produce more on-time deliveries. The cost
is in the action itself: with no model involved, moving aircraft and stock
instead of keeping to local rules loses about two percentage points at four
hubs and 13 at 64.
We test models as live managers, as vetoes over a replanner's orders, and
as authors of rules that run without them. Each arrangement still chooses
to relocate aircraft or transfer stock. Under the relocating policy and
in most live-manager and veto runs, the added missed requests concentrate
at hubs that gave aircraft or stock. One model moves little and lends
widely, but also underperforms. Telling a model what its past moves cost
does not reliably reduce them. Showing only hubs with problems
reduces moves for some models at 16 hubs but does not stop them at 64; a
reserve rule limits moves directly. All results are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robotic systems increasingly divide decisions across levels. A high-level
component chooses a goal or subtask, and a controller or learned policy
turns that choice into motion. Recent robot foundation models use this
structure to carry out longer tasks: $\pi_{0.5}$ predicts semantic subtasks
before low-level actions, and GR00T N1 combines reasoning about a task with
physical control \citep{black2025pi05,nvidia2025gr00t}. A team of robots
adds decisions about who should act, how work should be shared, and what to
do when the situation changes. Language models are attractive at this
level because an operator can describe a new task or disruption in ordinary
language while established software continues to dispatch and control the
robots.

Language-model planning for teams has taken several forms. SMART-LLM
breaks an instruction into tasks for groups of robots
\citep{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments to a mixed team
after feedback \citep{liu2025coherent}. Other systems compare centralized
and local planners \citep{chen2024scalable}, direct large collectives from
language \citep{vatnsdal2026compass}, or dispatch and reposition a fleet
of vehicles \citep{lyu2025llmoddr}. These systems show ways to connect a
model to a team of robots. A fleet that operates through a day poses a further
question: an order can solve the problem in front of the manager and still
leave the fleet less able to handle what happens next.
As the fleet grows, the manager's interface matters too: which parts of
the fleet should the model see, and which changes should it be allowed to
make?

We study these questions with a regional delivery fleet. Each depot, which we
call a hub, has its own aircraft and stock. Requests arrive throughout the
day, and a dispatcher assigns them to available vehicles. When urgent
requests surge across several hubs, a manager can change which requests
take priority, allow an aircraft from a neighboring hub to make a delivery
and return home, or move aircraft and stock to the affected hub. Each
choice may help with the surge. Moving aircraft and stock also leaves the
neighboring hub with less capacity when its next requests arrive. The
manager must decide whether the immediate help is worth that later cost.

Judging a plan only when the surge begins hides this later cost. The
same aircraft may serve another request after returning home, or it may
still be based elsewhere. Its route and return time determine which later
requests it can reach before their deadlines. We therefore compare
managers on the same stream of requests and disruptions, and score what
the vehicles flew and delivered over the complete mission. Two runs with
the same requests can still diverge if a model gives different replies.
Each order changes the fleet state seen at later decisions.

We built Ripple, a multi-agent simulator, to run these comparisons.
A manager gives orders to a dispatcher; vehicle autonomies, controllers,
and motion models carry them out on the same clock. Metrics follow the
flights and requests after each order, including work outside the immediate
disruption. We can change the manager, its view of the fleet, or the vehicle
model while running matched missions. Model replies are saved so each
observed mission can be inspected and replayed. The fixed-policy study runs
the same 8.5-hour mission with up to 5,120 aircraft. That scale lets us
measure how a change to one hub affects work elsewhere while requests keep
arriving across the region.

We first use Ripple to take a replanner's help apart one piece at a time.
In the regional missions studied here, local rules improve on-time
delivery, and letting aircraft serve neighboring hubs adds to that gain.
Moving aircraft and stock between hubs lowers on-time delivery, with a
larger loss as the region grows from four to 64 hubs, and the added missed
requests fall mainly at the hubs that gave. We then put a language model
in three roles: it gives the orders itself, reviews the replanner's orders
and drops the ones it rejects, or writes the rules once before the day
starts. Models in each role relocate aircraft or transfer stock. Most
losses under live managers and vetoes concentrate at giving hubs, though
one model mostly lends instead and still underperforms. Feedback about
past moves does not reliably reduce them; a reserve rule limits them
directly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Planning for robot teams}
Language models have been used to decompose tasks and assign them to teams
of heterogeneous robots. SMART-LLM forms coalitions for subtasks
\citep{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments after feedback
\citep{liu2025coherent}. Chen et al. compare centralized, decentralized,
and hybrid organizations as team size grows \citep{chen2024scalable}.
Scale-Plan filters a larger action graph before calling a model
\citep{gupta2026scaleplan}. COMPASS studies language-conditioned collective
behavior with up to 1,024 robots in short flocking trials
\citep{vatnsdal2026compass}. These systems ask how a team can carry out a
command. We ask what happens after a fleet manager changes the distribution
of vehicles and stock over a day, including at hubs it was not trying to
help.

\paragraph{Managing a fleet}
Dispatch and repositioning are coupled: a vehicle sent to one area is no
longer available in another. LLM-ODDR uses a language model to dispatch
orders and reposition drivers; its surge study uses 300 taxis
\citep{lyu2025llmoddr}. In aerial robotics, COLA executes multi-UAV
missions through collaborating agents \citep{ludwig2026cola}, and
Web-of-Drones connects a language agent to flight tools
\citep{iannoli2026webofdrones}. The delivery problem here has heterogeneous
vehicles, hub stock, deadlines, and rules that change during a mission.
We give the manager several ways to help, including temporary service by a
neighbor, and measure their later cost in the same operating day. Fixed
policies run with up to 5,120 aircraft; the model-managed comparisons use
80 and 320 aircraft.

\paragraph{Interfaces and checks}
The interface through which an agent observes and acts can change its
behavior without changing the model. SWE-agent shows this for software
agents \citep{yang2024sweagent}; work on tool documentation and context
selection studies particular parts of that interface
\citep{hsieh2023tooldoc,lodha2026lesscontext}. In robotics, SayCan grounds
skill selection in affordances \citep{ahn2022saycan}, while Safety Chip and
RoboGuard check proposed actions against constraints
\citep{yang2024safetychip,ravichandran2025roboguard}.
LLM-Modulo argues for verifiers around a model rather than treating its
output as an executable plan \citep{kambhampati2024llmmodulo}. Our checker
rejects infeasible or rule-breaking orders; a separate guard limits how
much capacity can leave any one hub. We compare those mechanisms with a
smaller view and with a model that reviews a replanner's orders.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:ripple}

Ripple runs a fleet whose vehicles and manager can be changed independently.
Its design follows SCRIMMAGE's separation of agents, plugins, and world
interactions
\citep{demarco2018}. Each agent owns physical state and a stack of plugins.
The engine advances every agent through the same ordered phases, so a fleet
decision, a vehicle's response, and a later request belong to one mission
history. A research crate supplies the agents and algorithms for a study;
the engine supplies timing, messages, recording, and metrics. The delivery
crate used here defines hubs, a dispatcher, couriers, manager orders, and
the score. The same engine can run a different autonomy or motion model
without changing the simulation loop.

\subsection{Composing a fleet}

An autonomy chooses what an agent does. A controller turns its commands
into motion inputs, and a motion model advances the physical state. Sensors
produce readings; estimators update the belief available to autonomy and
controllers. Interactions, networks, and metrics operate across agents.
The mission selects these components for each agent. In this study,
fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors share the request stream but use
different controllers and motion models. Fixed-wing aircraft make a
parachute drop on a pass; multirotors can also land on hospital rooftops.
A manager need only choose among actions the vehicle autonomies expose.

The delivery crate defines the vehicle behaviors for this study. Its
Courier autonomy handles the vehicle's lifecycle---idle, serving a
request, returning, reloading, relocating, or lost---and runs a delivery
skill for each assignment. The skill library describes which aircraft can
\texttt{drop} or \texttt{land} and supplies the behavior at the site; an
assignment can also include a waypoint around a closed area. The dispatcher
checks availability, skill, and estimated endurance before assigning a
request. A model can change fleet rules through manager orders, while the
dispatcher still selects a feasible skill and the courier flies it. The
score is based on that flight. A request that looks feasible from the
manager's summary can still become late while an aircraft reaches it and
returns for another.

\subsection{From a manager order to a flight}

The dispatcher assigns individual requests to vehicles. At each decision
it considers idle vehicles at the site's hub, stock, skill, estimated range,
and arrival time. A request it cannot assign is retried. This dispatcher
runs in every condition, including the condition with no manager. The
manager acts above it when an event begins or at a scheduled check-in. It
can change local priorities and restrictions, permit another hub to serve a
site, relocate aircraft, or transfer stock by flight. The manager never
assigns an individual request.

An order changes the dispatcher's future choices as well as the immediate
flight. \texttt{serve\_from} lets a neighboring hub serve a site, after
which the aircraft returns to its own hub. \texttt{reposition} flies an
idle aircraft to a new hub, which becomes its home. \texttt{transfer} uses
a vehicle to carry stock between hubs. The latter two consume aircraft
time and leave the hub that gave them less capacity for later requests.
This difference is central to the experiment: a move can solve the current
shortage and still reduce the day's on-time share. Ripple records each
order's acceptance and the subsequent flight, delivery, and hub state.

For the comparisons below, six fixed policies and three ways of using a
language model issue orders to the same dispatcher and couriers.
For a live model, the delivery crate writes a view of the fleet and active
events, calls the model, parses its reply into the same orders a scripted
policy can issue, and applies them. A model can instead review a
replanner's proposed orders and drop some, or write a policy file once
before the day. The latter runs without model calls during the mission.
The couriers still execute the same skills, and the metric scores their
flights by the same rule.

\subsection{Running matched missions}

Ripple's ordered phases put the manager and vehicles on the same timeline:
autonomy acts, controllers and motion models execute, sensors and world
interactions update, messages are routed, estimators update belief, and
metrics observe the result. The manager's view is formed from reported
fleet state and events rather than the physical truth used by the metric.
A closure, for example, is judged from a vehicle's actual position during
the closure window. A delivery whose flight broke an active rule does not
count as served, even if the manager ordered it in time.

Requests and events are drawn from random numbers keyed by hub and event
index. A seed therefore defines a common day for managers that give
different orders. Randomness in the simulator is keyed by purpose and
stable identities, so a run repeats exactly. We also save each model reply
with the prompt that produced it. An unchanged model run can then be
replayed without another call. These properties make it possible to
inspect a failed mission and to compare interventions on matched request
streams. A changed view or policy changes future prompts and may require
new model replies; saved replies
alone do not make different conditions identical.

The simulator carries the whole region between manager calls. In the
largest fixed-policy runs, it advances 5,120 aircraft through 306,000
steps of 0.1 seconds while requests, stock, and events change across the
region.
Requests arrive for the first eight hours of that 8.5-hour day. A run
with 1,280 aircraft and no model calls took about 16 minutes on one
worker, roughly 32 times faster than real time. Model inference is timed
separately from the simulator.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

We use Ripple to study fleet decisions on a day of regional medical
delivery. Hubs lie on a square grid 15~km apart (Figure~\ref{fig:region}).
Each hub has three fixed-wing aircraft, two multirotors, four blood-product
stocks, six clinics, and two hospitals with rooftops and nearby ground
pads. Delivery requests arrive at twelve per hub per hour, with more during
surges. Routine, urgent, and trauma requests have two-hour, 30-minute, and
15-minute deadlines. Requests arrive for eight hours, and the fleet has
30 minutes to finish the last of them. We compare fixed policies at 4,
16, and 64 hubs, or 20 to 320 aircraft, and also run them at 256 and
1,024 hubs. The model comparisons use 16 and 64 hubs. Within each
comparison, policies and models face matched requests and events; the
robustness checks deliberately change the workload or fleet.

The scale is that of a real operation. Zipline's long-range aircraft
cruises at 97~km/h and flies round trips of over 190~km
\citep{zipline2023factsheet}; our fixed-wing aircraft cruise at 90~km/h,
and the 15~km hub spacing and 4 to 13~km site distances sit well inside
that range.

Events include trauma surges, stock shortages, hub outages, closed airspace,
weather restrictions, priority changes, and rooftop closures. Their counts
are drawn per hub, so a larger region encounters more disruptions. The same
seed gives the same requests and events to every manager, so each
comparison is on matched days. The base fleet uses a kinematic fixed-wing
aircraft and a point multirotor with limited speed and endurance. Ripple
also has checked six-degree-of-freedom fixed-wing and multirotor models;
the results here do not establish that the manager ranking persists under
those models.

The outcome is the on-time share of requests, weighted one for routine,
two for urgent, and four for trauma. We also report late and missed
requests, lost vehicles, rule breaches, accepted and refused orders, moves
of aircraft or stock, and lending permissions. A \emph{move} is an applied
relocation or stock transfer; a \emph{lend} is an applied permission for a
neighboring hub to serve a site, not necessarily a completed flight. The
score is computed from deliveries and trajectories, not from the manager's
intent.

Six fixed policies, rules written once and applied the same way all day,
set the bar. \emph{No manager} is the dispatcher alone. \emph{Local rules}
adds orders that change only what a hub's own aircraft do: priorities,
closures, grounding, and ground-pad delivery. \emph{Lending} adds
permission for a neighboring hub to serve a site, after which the aircraft
comes home. \emph{Relocating} adds aircraft moves and stock transfers to
local rules, without lending. The \emph{replanner} uses every order and
also tops up low stock at check-ins. \emph{Return} is the replanner with
its relocated aircraft sent home once the event ends. The policies give
the same orders through the same dispatcher and are compared on the same
days.

A language model takes three roles. As the \emph{live manager} it is
called when an event begins and every 30 minutes, reads a view of the
fleet, and gives orders. As a \emph{veto} it reads the replanner's proposed
orders and drops the ones it rejects, but adds none. As the \emph{author of
the rules} it writes a policy file once, before the day, which then runs
with no further calls. Four models, Claude Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus and
GPT-6 Luna, are compared in the first two roles, on three seeds at 16 hubs
and two at 64. The comparisons of what the model sees and the policy
writing use Haiku and Sonnet. Every model call used one frozen system
prompt and one text per event kind; the prompt, the texts, one complete
call and one veto with their replies, and the per-run and per-seed numbers
are in the appendix, in the
repository.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/EthanMBoos/ripple/tree/main/paper/appendix}}

We ask three questions. First, how much on-time delivery does relocating
aircraft cost, when no model is involved? Second, knowing that cost, does
a language model still relocate aircraft, in each of its three roles:
giving the orders, vetoing a planner's orders, or writing the rules?
Third, what stops a model from relocating? Fourth, what should a model
see and be allowed to order when the fleet reaches thousands of aircraft?
We set up this fleet API question below; the model runs that will answer
it are planned.

3~km off the grid changes each policy's mean by less than two points.
Increasing the fleet from five to twelve aircraft per hub narrows the
replanner's deficit to about two points at 16 and 64 hubs, without
reversing the ranking.

Figure~\ref{fig:region} shows the mechanism on one surge: the surge is
served about equally well, but a hub that gave aircraft later misses its
own requests. Figure~\ref{fig:loss} shows that pattern over whole days.
Under the relocating policy at 16 hubs, giving hubs have 154 more late or
undelivered requests per day than under local rules; receiving hubs recover
seven, leaving 147 more across the region. At 64 hubs, giving hubs lose
16 additional requests per hundred of their own, while receiving hubs
serve two more per hundred.

Can a longer surge repay the move? On otherwise quiet 16-hub days, we
held one five-hub surge for 15, 60, 120, or 240 minutes at the same
request rate, using five matched seeds at each length. At 15 minutes,
relocating scores 0.87 against local rules' 0.94; lending and return
both score about 0.96. At 60 minutes, lending, the replanner, and Return
beat local rules on all five seeds; Return scores 0.73 and the replanner
0.69 against local rules' 0.65, while relocating without lending still
trails at 0.63. At 120 minutes all four alternatives beat local rules on
all five seeds; at 240, none has a higher mean. Moving can pay in the one-
and two-hour surges on this quiet day, especially when aircraft return.
The main comparison has 15-minute surges, where the move costs more than
it gains.

the region, and the models act on it.

\subsection{What stops a model from relocating}
\label{sec:holds}

We tested Haiku and Sonnet as live managers on two matched 16-hub days,
changing the view and the checks one at a time. With a summary of every
hub, Haiku makes 44 to 52 moves and Sonnet 37 to 66. Reporting the
outcome of the last orders reduces Haiku's moves on one seed, but Sonnet
moves more on both. A feasibility checker and a line showing what the
giving hub kept do not consistently reduce moves either. Information
about past costs does not by itself stop the next move.

A reserve rule does. It refuses orders that would leave a hub with too
few aircraft or too little stock. With this guard, Haiku makes 10 to 25
moves and Sonnet 28 to 38; 29 to 38 orders are refused per day. The
same guard reduces the replanner to 20 or 21 moves, with no model
involved. Neither guarded model consistently beats local rules.

Showing only hubs with problems reduces Haiku to seven moves on each
16-hub day without improving its score; Sonnet still makes 36 and 48.
This smaller view is the setting of Table~\ref{tab:models}. At 64 hubs,
all tested models still relocate; there are dozens of problem hubs at
each call. A smaller view can change behavior; it does not enforce a
reserve.

\section{The Fleet API at Scale}
\label{sec:scale}

The fleet API has two parts: what the model sees and what it can order.
Its report can describe every hub, show only hubs with problems, or cover
one cluster. Its orders can set local rules, lend an aircraft for one
flight, or move aircraft and stock. The report sets the size of the
model's task; the orders set how far its decisions can reach.

The completed runs show why both parts matter. At 16 hubs an exceptions
report holds Haiku to seven moves on each tested day, while Sonnet still
moves dozens of times. At 64 hubs all four models move under that report.
Showing less changes what the model chooses, while a reserve guard changes
which of its orders are accepted. The fixed policies at 256 and 1,024
hubs, with 1,280 and 5,120 aircraft, put the cost of moving at 13 points
at both sizes (Table~\ref{tab:policies}).

\emph{Planned.} At those two sizes, each model will manage one cluster of
16 hubs. It will see that cluster and may order changes within it,
including moves; orders naming another cluster will be refused. One
condition adds a manager above the clusters. It sees a summary of each
cluster and may move aircraft and stock between them. We will compare
these APIs on matched days with Haiku and one stronger model, using two
seeds per size. We will report on-time score, missed requests at hubs that
gave, moves per hundred hubs, model calls, and input tokens. The question
is whether a bounded local API helps the region without losing the
giving hubs' later work, and what changes when the manager can make moves
across the region.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The fixed policies give the model results a physical explanation. A manager
can help a stressed hub by changing priorities or by letting a neighbor's
aircraft serve its sites. Those actions expire or end with the flight.
Relocating an aircraft changes the giving hub's capacity for the rest of the
day, and a stock transfer uses an aircraft that could have delivered. In the
main mission this second kind of help costs more than it gains from 16 hubs
on, and the gap levels off past 64 hubs. On a quieter day with one longer
surge, returning aircraft helps at one and two hours, but no moving policy
has a higher mean than local rules at four hours. Models in every role
choose moves, so the result is more than one model making one bad call.

The interfaces change different parts of this behavior. Outcome feedback
and a line showing the giving hub's state give information, but they do
not prevent moves. A view of only the problem hubs holds some models back
at 16 hubs; at 64 the problems are plentiful and every tested model
relocates. A guard on each hub's reserve refuses orders that cross it,
and it helps the replanner as much as the model. A veto can remove orders
from a planner, but these models also remove useful lending permissions
while retaining some relocations. One next design would let the model
propose an intervention and have a planner price its cost over the rest
of the day before committing it. Ripple can
compare that design with the present ones on the same missions.

The study has clear limits. Its request distribution, event rates, hub
geometry, and eight-hour horizon are simulated. The reported fleets use
kinematic vehicle models, so the result concerns fleet allocation under
these flight times and ranges; it does not establish a difference caused by
six-degree-of-freedom dynamics. The model comparisons have three seeds at
16 hubs and two at 64. The four models were reached through two
command-line tools that wrap them differently, one keeping a coding
agent's tools and one not; calling the model APIs directly would make the
cleaner cross-model comparison. Two model runs in 40 matched or beat local
rules, so the claim is about the average and the spread of a model's days,
not a bound. Different demand patterns, a few hubs busy all day beside
quiet ones, and policies that return aircraft sooner would test where
relocating begins to pay.

Ripple's broader role is to make these questions experimental. The manager
can be changed without rewriting the courier, the vehicle model without
rewriting the manager, and the score can follow the consequences of each
choice through a complete day. The important outcome here is not a
universal instruction to avoid relocation. It is evidence that a fleet
manager's apparently helpful action needs to be judged by the work it
displaces later and elsewhere.
