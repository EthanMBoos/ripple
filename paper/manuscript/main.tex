\begin{abstract}
Language models are now routinely proposed as high-level planners for robot
fleets, calling a library of skills as tools. This lets an operator give new
instructions while the vehicles retain their own controllers and dynamics.
It also raises a question that a single plan cannot answer: how does the
planner behave across repeated decisions and through the vehicles that
execute its calls? We present Ripple, a multi-agent simulator for evaluating
language-model fleet planners over complete missions. Planners, skills,
controllers, and motion models can be connected and varied independently;
keyed randomness and saved model replies make an unchanged mission exactly
replayable. In an initial mixed-fleet delivery study on five test missions
with two replies each, Claude Haiku broke mid-mission rules in 7--10\% of
eligible decisions and in 6--9 of 10 complete runs. For both the grounding
and standby rules, one plain wording was never broken and another, as plain,
almost always was. Adding a waypoint skill removed the observed no-fly
breaches. Ripple reruns the same missions with the rule's wording, the skills
on offer, or the vehicle model changed, and checks every rule from the
flight. All results are from simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robotic systems increasingly divide decisions across timescales. A high-level
component chooses a goal or subtask, and a controller or learned policy turns
that choice into motion. This hierarchy appears in recent robot foundation
models, where a model predicts semantic subtasks before producing actions
\cite{black2025pi05,nvidia2025gr00t}. A team of robots adds another choice:
which robot should act? Language models have been used to assign tasks,
select skills, and coordinate teams with different capabilities
\cite{kannan2024smartllm,liu2025coherent,li2026llmmrs}. Their appeal is that
an operator can describe a new request or constraint in ordinary language,
and the planner can call skills already implemented on the robots. A skill
library gives the planner a way to use new behaviors and different vehicles
through a common interface.

That interface joins decisions made from language to behavior in the physical
world. A skill call has a name and arguments, but its effect also depends on
the receiving vehicle, its controller, and the path it actually follows.
The connection matters throughout a mission. A robot finishes one task and
becomes available for another; new requests and operating rules arrive while
other robots are still moving. An instruction given once may need to govern
many subsequent calls, including calls made when a deadline approaches or a
preferred vehicle is unavailable. The planner must choose actions that are
appropriate both for the current task and for the system that will carry
them out. How can we tell whether it keeps doing so as the mission unfolds?

Consider a fleet delivering medical supplies, as drones already do in Rwanda
\cite{nisingizwe2022drone}. Fixed-wing aircraft cover long routes quickly
but cannot land on a hospital rooftop; multirotors can land but travel more
slowly. A planner assigns requests by calling skills such as drop and land.
While both kinds of aircraft are serving requests, operations may close a
clinic, restrict a region of airspace, or ground one vehicle. The instruction
is legible to a language model, but following it can require more than
recognizing its words. A straight flight to a clinic may cross the restricted
region. A vehicle may lack the skill or range to take a different route. The
next assignment may face a larger backlog and less time before a delivery is
late. The same planner, skill library, and fleet must handle each change.

These conditions make the unit of evaluation consequential. A planner can
follow a rule at one decision and break it at the next. Even an infrequent
slip gives a mission with many decisions several chances to fail. Some rules
can be judged from the call itself, while others depend on the flight or
delivery that follows it. A proposed route and the trajectory flown by a
vehicle can differ. A single prompt tests one decision under an instruction;
a complete mission tests whether the planner continues to follow it as
requests, vehicles, and deadlines change.

Studying this behavior also requires controlled comparisons. Model replies
can vary when the prompt is held fixed
\cite{he2025nondeterminism,atil2024nondeterminism}, and the fleet studies
closest to ours report few runs per configuration
\cite{ludwig2026cola,iannoli2026webofdrones,zhang2026multiuavplat}.
Changing a rule, a skill, or a vehicle model can change the next flight and
the state seen at every later decision. To understand such changes, we need
matched request streams and scenario seeds, a record of the model's replies,
and a way to check what each vehicle did. Those records should also let us
revisit a failure without paying for another model run.

We built Ripple for these comparisons. It is a multi-agent simulator in
which planners, vehicle autonomies, controllers, motion models, and metrics
are separate plugins. A planner calls skills hosted by each vehicle's
autonomy; the controller and dynamics then determine the flight. The same
planner can be connected to another skill library or vehicle model, and
rules can be checked against flights and deliveries. Keyed randomness keeps
scenarios reproducible across parallel runs. Saving every model reply makes
an unchanged mission exactly replayable, so we can inspect failures again
and compare conditions on matched request streams and scenario seeds.

We use a mixed-fleet delivery study to show what this reveals. In an initial
evaluation with Claude Haiku, rules were broken in fewer than one eligible
decision in ten, yet in most complete runs. For both the grounding and
standby rules, one plain wording was never broken and another, as plain,
almost always was. In the mission of Fig.~1, the planner holds
an urgent delivery to respect a no-fly zone, then sends an aircraft through
it as the deadline approaches. Offering a waypoint skill removed the
observed no-fly breaches; leaving vehicle limits out of the prompt led to
repeated fixed-wing landing calls.

Our contributions are Ripple and a first study made with it. In Ripple a
language-model planner calls skills hosted on fixed-wing aircraft and
multirotors, each flown by its own controller and dynamics, and a whole
mission, the model's replies included, replays exactly. The same missions
can be rerun with the rule's wording, the skills on offer, or the vehicle
model changed, and whether a rule was kept is checked from the flight, not
the plan.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language models as planners for robot teams}
Language models can turn broad instructions into assignments for teams of
robots. SMART-LLM decomposes a task and forms coalitions of agents
\cite{kannan2024smartllm}; COHERENT revises assignments after feedback from
quadrotors, quadrupeds, and arms \cite{liu2025coherent}. Other systems study
communication and coordination among agents with different capabilities
\cite{mandi2024roco,chen2024scalable,yang2025autohmallm,li2026llmmrs}.
Aerial work brings these decisions closer to flight: Web-of-Drones keeps an
agent in a long-running loop over ArduPilot tools
\cite{iannoli2026webofdrones}, COLA turns commands into calls for VTOL
aircraft \cite{ludwig2026cola}, and TACOS places trajectory planning and
supervision behind swarm commands \cite{nazzari2025tacos}. MultiUAV-Plat
takes a complementary route to scale, with 1,500 tasks and hidden checks
over a lightweight UAV interface, leaving richer flight dynamics for future
work \cite{zhang2026multiuavplat}. Together these studies show how a language
model can coordinate a team, connect to flight software, or be evaluated over
many tasks. We study the junction of these concerns: one planner makes
repeated assignments in a fleet of fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors,
while instructions change and the vehicles' flights determine whether those
instructions were kept.

\paragraph{Language models over skills}
Skill libraries give a language planner a way to act through existing robot
behaviors. SayCan selects described skills using both language likelihood
and learned affordance values \cite{ahn2022saycan}; Code as Policies writes
programs over a perception and control API \cite{liang2023codeaspolicies}.
RoboGen chooses learned or classical methods for different subtasks
\cite{wang2024robogen}, while Voyager adds executable behaviors to its
library as it works \cite{wang2023voyager}. Other systems pass plans to a
solver or verifier rather than relying on the model for every step
\cite{liu2023llmp,chen2023autotamp,kambhampati2024llmmodulo}.
Tool descriptions affect how models use an interface
\cite{hsieh2023tooldoc}, and selecting the right skill becomes harder as
libraries grow \cite{chen2026skillscaling}. This work establishes selection,
composition, and growth as distinct design choices. Our study varies the
skills offered to the same fleet planner under the same operating rule. In
particular, we test whether offering a route through a chosen waypoint
changes compliance with a no-fly instruction over a complete mission.

\paragraph{Following rules}
Agents have also been tested on rules that change during a task. House Rules
adds timed natural-language updates to text-world tasks and measures whether
agents respect the active specification; indirect wording raises violation
rates, and a symbolic shield prevents forbidden actions
\cite{vimalkanth2026houserules}. The Safety Chip translates language rules
into temporal-logic constraints and filters an LLM agent's proposed actions;
its evaluation includes VirtualHome and a real robot
\cite{yang2024safetychip}. Work on long conversations finds that reliability
can fall when a task unfolds over several turns \cite{laban2025lost}.
These studies distinguish following a rule from having a mechanism that
enforces it. Our planner sees every active rule in every fresh prompt, so the
question is whether it chooses compliant calls repeatedly as the mission
changes. We measure breaches both per eligible decision and per complete
mission, checking rules against flights and deliveries. We also compare
ordinary skill calls with a waypoint skill and with a check that refuses
breaking calls.

\paragraph{Controlled mission evaluation}
Learning results depend on seeds and evaluation choices, motivating repeated
trials \cite{agarwal2021precipice,gorsane2022evaluation}. Agent benchmarks
likewise report variation across attempts
\cite{yao2024taubench,bjarnason2026randomness}.
For a robot fleet, a small change in a flight can also change which vehicles
are available and what the planner sees next. Simulator execution therefore
matters to a matched comparison. MuJoCo scopes its reproducibility guarantee
to one version and architecture \cite{mujocodocs}; Isaac Lab documents
sensitivity to execution order \cite{isaaclabdocs}, and GPUSimBench measures
variation across parallel environments \cite{zhang2026gpusimbench}. Ripple
uses keyed randomness and a fixed phase order to run complete missions with
matched requests and events, producing identical output across worker
counts. We retain model replies to inspect each observed run. Matched worlds
and repeated model samples then let us measure how instructions, skills, and
vehicle models affect whole-mission outcomes while accounting for planner
variation.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:system}

Ripple runs the planner on the same clock as the vehicles it directs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). In our study, the dispatcher reads waiting requests,
vehicle reports, the current instruction, and descriptions of the available
skills. It returns a vehicle, request, and skill, with a waypoint if the
skill needs one. The vehicle's Courier autonomy runs that skill on subsequent
ticks; its controller and motion model turn the skill's targets into a
flight. Deliveries, airspace entries, and vehicle availability follow from
that flight. The dispatcher sees the changed fleet at its next decision, so
a choice early in the mission can change the requests and vehicles available
to it later.

\subsection{Composing a fleet}

Each agent carries an autonomy that decides what to do, a controller that
turns its commands into motion inputs, a motion model that advances the
vehicle, and optional sensors and estimators. Interaction, network, and
metrics plugins act on the world. A mission selects these components for
each agent, so a fixed-wing aircraft and a multirotor can share requests and
airspace while using different controllers and dynamics. The interfaces
carry typed values with units and frames, checked where components connect.
The simulation engine supplies the clock, state ownership, messages, and
phase order; the research plugin supplies the vehicle behavior or fleet
decision. It does not prescribe a dispatch algorithm or a skill vocabulary.

In our study, the dispatcher is an autonomy on the depot, and each vehicle
has a Courier autonomy. The dispatcher assigns work, while a Courier keeps
its own lifecycle: idle, executing a skill, returning, reloading, or lost.
The same Courier and skill library run above the fixed-wing or multirotor
controller. Replacing the fixed-wing kinematic model with a 6-DOF aircraft
and its autopilot changes how an assignment flies without changing the
planner's call or the Courier's skill logic. This is the component boundary
we use when testing whether a planning result depends on the vehicle model.

\subsection{From a skill call to a flight}

Ripple's skill pattern lives within the vehicle's autonomy. The autonomy
owns the vehicle's lifecycle and command ports, while each skill owns the
state and targets of one behavior. A planner can therefore choose among
behaviors without competing plugins trying to control the same vehicle.
The engine supplies the autonomy interface, timing, and messages; a research
crate defines the skill contract appropriate to its task. In our delivery
crate, Courier hosts skills with a description shown to the planner,
parameters for a call, a target to pursue each tick, and a condition for
completion. A mission chooses which library entries the planner may use.

The dispatcher builds a prompt from waiting requests, vehicle status,
limits when provided, active instructions, and descriptions of the offered
skills (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt} shows one in full). A reply
names a vehicle, request, and skill, with any skill-specific parameters. The
dispatcher checks that the vehicle is idle, the skill fits the site and
vehicle, and the estimated round trip fits its remaining endurance. It
records refused calls. In the unguarded study conditions, it does not
enforce the natural-language instruction; compliance is judged from the
subsequent execution.

After a call is accepted, Courier starts the named skill and advances it on
every tick. The skill chooses a target; Courier converts that target into
commands for the vehicle's controller, and the motion model determines the
path actually flown. For example, \texttt{drop\_via} first targets a waypoint
chosen by the planner, then the delivery site, then the waypoint on the way
home. It uses the same vehicle controllers as \texttt{drop}; offering it adds
a route choice to the planner without changing the request stream or the
no-fly rule. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows an actual call and trajectory. The
description and capability flags sit beside the skill's behavior; the
dispatcher reads those flags when checking calls. Adding a skill changes
the Courier's library without changing Ripple's simulation loop.

\subsection{Running controlled missions}

A mission advances in ordered phases: autonomy decides, controllers and
motion models execute each substep, sensors produce readings, interactions
and networks act, estimators update belief, and metrics score the result.
Every agent finishes a phase before the next begins. Motion models update
physical state, while autonomy and controllers act on belief; the planner's
choice therefore enters the same loop as every other agent decision. A
delivery changes vehicle availability and later assignments, so changing one
call may change the state seen at many later decisions. The unit of
comparison is the completed mission, with its sequence of calls, flights,
deliveries, and rule outcomes.

Ripple fixes two sources of variation in that comparison. First, each
random draw is keyed by its purpose---the seed, agent, plugin, tick, and a
stable key supplied by the plugin---rather than by draw order. A sensor's
noise for a given contact can therefore stay the same in paired runs even
if another contact appears in only one run. Second, each phase finishes
before another begins, so worker scheduling does not change the result.
An unchanged mission has byte-identical output at one, two, or eight
workers. A sweep can run many seeds and component choices in parallel while
preserving these matched worlds.

The dispatcher revisits its plan as requests arrive and vehicles return.
Each prompt describes the current mission without the model's earlier
replies; every active instruction appears again. This keeps conversational
memory outside the test and gives a rule multiple opportunities to affect a
decision. We save the model's replies so an observed mission replays exactly
without another model call. Changing a rule or skill changes the prompt and
may require new replies, while the scenario seed and event stream remain
matched. Repeated live samples then measure the planner's own variation
separately from variation in the simulated world.

\subsection{Execution checks}

\prov A 60~s mission with three Rascals on the 6-DOF model under their
autopilots, at 100 steps per second, runs in about 40~ms on one core of an
Apple M2 Pro, including writing the recording.

Ripple's numerical models grew out of SCRIMMAGE \cite{demarco2018}; we use
its implementation as a check on the models and phase timing rather than as
the paper's subject. Over 100 generated scenarios, the two agree to
$2.8\times10^{-11}$~m, and the fixed-wing aircraft and its autopilot agree
to $2.3\times10^{-13}$~m. These checks show that the equations and timing
came through intact. They do not establish agreement with a real aircraft.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}

Our task is medical delivery by a mixed fleet (Fig.~1). Two
fixed-wing aircraft and two multirotors share one depot, which serves six
clinics 3--13~km away and two hospital rooftops nearby. The fixed-wing
aircraft are fast and fly the long legs, dropping packages by parachute on a
pass; the multirotors are slower but can hover, so they can also land, and
only they can serve a hospital rooftop. Fixed-wing aircraft fly a kinematic
model with a minimum speed and a limited turn rate under a PID autopilot,
and multirotors a point model capped at 12~m/s. Over a 30-minute mission,
twenty requests open, some of them urgent, and the planner decides which
vehicle takes each one. We also repeat the no-fly missions with the
fixed-wing aircraft on a 6-DOF model under its autopilot to check whether
the results depend on the kinematic model.

Partway through the mission, operations may send one instruction in plain
language: a clinic closes, a no-fly zone opens, a multirotor has to stay on
standby, or a vehicle is grounded. In the unguarded conditions, the planner
alone decides whether to follow it. We write each instruction several ways
and check, from the fleet's flights and deliveries, whether it was obeyed.
The check is on the fleet, not on the call, and is the same whatever the
wording says: time a vehicle spends inside the no-fly zone during its
window, time the grounded vehicle is away, and time in the window with no
idle multirotor at the depot. A multirotor sent out just before the standby
window and still away when it opens therefore breaks the rule, though the
call that sent it was made before the window.

We score a mission by the share of requests delivered on time, and report
broken instructions, refused calls, and vehicles lost for lack of endurance
beside it. Every comparison is made on the same missions.

The wordings and scoring were written on three development missions, before
any test mission ran. Each rule has two wordings: A, written first, and B,
the one broken most often in development. The results below come from five
new test missions, each run with two independent Claude Haiku reply
samples. Two references frame the comparisons: the same missions without the
instruction, for what keeping a rule costs, and the planner with breaking
calls refused in code, which keeps every rule by construction. Our
experiments focus on the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Does the planner follow rules nobody programmed, and what does
  following them cost?
\item When it slips, does the right skill help more than better wording or
  a check in code?
\item Does the planner give a vehicle a task that vehicle can't fly?
\end{enumerate}
As a control, we also measure how much a planner's score varies between live
reruns of the same missions. \stub{More models, including an open-weights
one, and the number of prompt versions tried.}

\subsection{Does the planner follow rules nobody programmed?}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig-slope.tex}
\caption{\textbf{Dependable one decision at a time, not over a mission.} For
each rule and wording, the share of decisions that broke the rule where it
could be broken (left) and the share of the ten missions broken (right), on
one scale; with ten missions each, the right-hand shares are good to about 25
to 30 points (95\% intervals in Appendix~\ref{app:counts}). Slips in 7--10\% of decisions break 60--90\%
of missions. For the grounded vehicle and the standby rule, wording A was
never broken while wording B, as plain, almost always was ($p < 0.001$
each, Fisher's exact test on missions broken); the no-fly zone was broken as
often under either wording.}
\label{fig:slips}
\end{figure}

To answer Question 1, we gave the planner three of the instructions (the
no-fly zone, the grounded vehicle, and the multirotor on standby), each in
two wordings: the one written first (A), and the one broken most often in
development (B). Clinic closures were never broken in development and are left
out. Each mission is compared with the same mission run without the
instruction. To see how the planner does one decision at a time, we also
took ten moments per rule from the test missions at which the rule could be
broken, kept each prompt exactly, swapped in each wording, and asked again,
twice per moment.

Fig.~\ref{fig:slips} shows the results; the counts are in
Appendix~\ref{app:counts}. One decision at a time, the planner looks
dependable: it broke a rule in at most one reply in ten (no replies under
wording A for the grounded vehicle and the standby rule, 2 of 20 under B, and
1 of 20 for the no-fly zone under either). Over whole missions, it is not. A
mission offers 7 to 22 decisions at which a rule can be broken, and if each
slipped independently at the measured rate $p$, a mission with $n$ of them
would be broken with probability $1-(1-p)^n$; this predicts the observed share
to within two missions in ten, a little low for the rules that offer the most
chances. For the grounded vehicle and the multirotor on standby, wording A was
kept in every decision of every mission, while wording B, just as plain, was
broken in 8 and 9 of 10 missions ($p < 0.001$ for each, Fisher's exact test on
missions broken). The no-fly zone was broken in 6 of 10 missions under either
wording. We suspect the reason is what the rule asks of the planner: the
other two name the vehicle or the act to avoid, while the no-fly rule names
only a place, and keeping it means working out that a straight route to a
clinic crosses that place, which neither wording does for it. The skill that
makes the route explicit removed every no-fly breach (Sec.~\ref{sec:skill}),
which fits this reading, but we have not tested it directly. Breaks lean toward urgent deliveries: 32 of the 37 breaking calls
served an urgent request, against two thirds of the calls that kept a rule,
with a median of 7 minutes to spare against 10. Following a rule also costs
deliveries: the grounded vehicle's wording A, kept in every mission, cost the
most, 10 points of on-time share.

\subsection{Does the right skill help the planner comply?}
\label{sec:skill}
To answer Question 2, we offered the planner \texttt{drop\_via}, a drop flown
out and back through a waypoint it chooses, alongside \texttt{drop} and
\texttt{land} under the no-fly zone, and compared the same missions without
it and with breaking calls refused in code. With \texttt{drop\_via} offered,
no decision broke the rule under either wording (0 of 53 each), against 8 of
84 and 7 of 73 without it, and no mission was broken against 6 of 10
($p = 0.011$ for each wording), at about the same on-time share (0.50 and
0.47, against 0.46 and 0.48). Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the call in
the mission of Fig.~1: offered the skill, the planner routes
the urgent delivery around the zone the moment it arrives. Refusing breaking
calls in code removed every breach of every rule, refusing 26 calls, at
little cost. A skill that makes the right action easy did as much as the
better wording, and for the no-fly zone, where no wording worked, more.
\stub{Planned: remove skills, and vary how they are described.}

\subsection{Does the planner know what each vehicle can do?}
To answer Question 3, we count calls that give a vehicle a task it cannot
fly, with the vehicles' limits stated in the prompt and left out, in the
missions without an instruction. With the limits stated, no call over ten
missions sent a vehicle on a skill it cannot run. With them left out, the
check refused 179 calls that sent a fixed-wing aircraft to land, about 18 a
mission, and the on-time share fell from 0.50 to 0.41. The planner needs to
be told what each vehicle can do.

With the fixed-wing aircraft on the 6-DOF model and its autopilot, the no-fly
zone was still broken, in 2 and 5 of 10 missions under wordings A and B,
against 6 and 6 on the kinematic model, at similar on-time shares (0.45 and
0.46, against 0.46 and 0.48). Neither difference is significant ($p = 0.17$
and $p = 1.0$): the breaches do not depend on the simpler model. The
multirotors stay on a point model; a multirotor's path is close to the
straight line the point model flies, the fixed-wing aircraft is the vehicle
whose path departs from it most, and the grounding and standby rules depend
on which vehicle flies, not how.

\subsection{Is a planner's score noise?}
As a control, we reran the same missions live. The world is identical
between reruns, so any difference comes from the model. \prov In the first
pilot, three live reruns of Claude Haiku over ten missions differed by 0.008
in mean on-time share. \stub{Repeat on the test missions.}

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

Ripple connects a planner's skill calls to the flights and later decisions
they produce. Our first study shows why that connection matters. Claude
Haiku usually followed an operating rule at any one eligible decision, yet
repeated decisions broke the rule in many complete missions. Under both
wordings of an explicit no-fly instruction, aircraft entered the restricted
region in 6 of 10 missions. In one trace, the planner held an urgent request
to respect the rule, then sent an aircraft through the region as its deadline
approached. A low error rate per call therefore offers little assurance for
a mission with many calls. A language model that reads an instruction should
not be the sole enforcer of a flight constraint.

The interventions point to two distinct roles for the system around the
planner. Offering \texttt{drop\_via} gave it a route around the zone and
removed the observed no-fly breaches at similar on-time delivery.
Refusing calls that would break a rule prevented every observed breach by
construction, at little cost to delivery. A skill can make a compliant
choice available, while a check on proposed calls can reject a known rule
violation when the planner slips. Breaks were more common on
urgent requests with less time to spare, which is consistent with pressure
from deadlines; these runs do not isolate deadline pressure as the cause.

This is a small simulated study of one model and selected instructions. The
fixed-wing comparison checks behavior against another implementation, not
against flight data, and the multirotors still use a point model. We next
need more missions and planners, controlled changes to deadline slack, and
tests of how skill descriptions and enforcement affect both compliance and
delivery. Flying the same calls on a small fleet would test whether the
failure modes survive real vehicle dynamics and sensing. Ripple's skill
interface also allows a learned behavior beside a classical one, making it
possible to ask whether a more capable executor changes the planner's
whole-mission reliability.
