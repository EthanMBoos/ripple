\begin{abstract}
Language models are now routinely proposed as the high-level planner for
robot fleets, calling a library of skills as tools. They are evaluated on
homogeneous fleets, with idealized execution, on runs that cannot be
repeated, and rarely against classical allocators. We present Ripple, a
deterministic multi-agent simulator in which whole episodes, the model's
replies included, replay byte for byte at any number of threads, fixed-wing
aircraft and multirotors share one world, and every skill is a plugin with
swappable classical and learned versions. With it we measure four things on
identical episodes: how much of a planner's score is noise, how often
planners give a vehicle a task its dynamics rule out, how success changes as
the skill library grows and shrinks, and where a language model beats an
optimizer and an auction. The task is medical delivery by a fleet of
fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors. \textit{[findings]}. We also re-run the
tasks of a recent drone-swarm study on mixed fleets. All results are from
simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Robot foundation models split the work in two: a slow planner decides in
language what to do next, and a fast policy does it. $\pi_{0.5}$ predicts
``pick up the pillow'' from ``clean the bedroom'' before producing motor
commands \cite{black2025pi05}, and GR00T N1 pairs a reasoning model with a
fast action model in the same way \cite{nvidia2025gr00t}. The same split
suits a fleet of vehicles. A language model reads requests and decides who
does what, and each vehicle's autonomy and autopilot carry it out. Language
models have been proposed for exactly this
\cite{li2026llmmrs,kannan2024smartllm,liu2025coherent,zhang2026multiuavplat}.

The planner's actions are calls to skills: fly a route, drop a package,
land, loiter. These are solved classical behaviours, and a learned one can
sit beside them where it helps. Consider a fleet delivering blood to
clinics, as fixed-wing drones do in Rwanda \cite{nisingizwe2022drone}. An
urgent request arrives for a distant clinic, and another for a hospital
rooftop nearby. A fixed-wing aircraft covers the long leg fastest and drops
its package on a pass, but it cannot hover, has a minimum speed, and needs
room to turn; only a multirotor can land on the roof. Existing planners
describe a robot's
abilities as a list of discrete skills
\cite{kannan2024smartllm,liu2025coherent}, which says nothing about these
limits. \emph{Do planners know what each vehicle can do, and where does a
language model earn its cost over a classical allocator?}

Answering this is harder than it looks, because the evaluations do not
repeat. A model at temperature zero still gives different replies from run
to run, enough to reorder a leaderboard \cite{he2025nondeterminism,atil2024nondeterminism}, and the fleet studies closest to ours report
a handful of flights or a single pass over their tasks
\cite{ludwig2026cola,iannoli2026webofdrones,zhang2026multiuavplat}. Ripple
keys every random number by what it is for, gives the same bytes at any
number of threads, and, with the model's replies cached by prompt, replays a
whole episode exactly. Two planners can then be compared on the same
episodes, and a planner's own variation measured apart from the world's.

Our contributions are Ripple as an instrument for evaluating language-model
fleet planners, and four measurements made with it: how much of a planner's
score is noise; how often planners give a vehicle a task its dynamics rule
out, on mixed fixed-wing and multirotor fleets; how success changes as the
skill library grows and shrinks, and when a learned skill replaces its
classical twin; and where a language model beats an optimizer and an
auction. \stub{One or two sentences of findings, once they exist.}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Language models as planners for robot teams}
Language models assign subtasks by skill \cite{kannan2024smartllm},
negotiate plans among robots \cite{mandi2024roco}, and coordinate
heterogeneous teams through rounds of feedback
\cite{chen2024scalable,liu2025coherent}, mostly in indoor simulators
\cite{li2026llmmrs}. Heterogeneity there means different discrete skills,
not different dynamics. For drones, language models call tools over
ArduPilot \cite{iannoli2026webofdrones,ludwig2026cola} and PX4
\cite{nazzari2025tacos}, plan choreographies corrected by a safety filter
\cite{schuck2025swarmgpt}, and are benchmarked at scale on MultiUAV-Plat,
whose 1{,}500 tasks leave flight dynamics out of scope
\cite{zhang2026multiuavplat}. The studies with real autopilots are small, the
large one is idealized, and every fleet is of one vehicle type. We found no
prior work that evaluates a language-model planner on a mixed fixed-wing and
multirotor fleet.

\paragraph{Language models over skills}
Language models choose among a robot's described skills
\cite{ahn2022saycan}, write programs that call them
\cite{liang2023codeaspolicies}, grow libraries of them
\cite{wang2023voyager}, pick between a learned and a classical skill for each
subtask \cite{wang2024robogen}, and hand problems to classical planners
\cite{liu2023llmp,chen2023autotamp,kambhampati2024llmmodulo}. Documentation
alone lets a model use new tools \cite{hsieh2023tooldoc}.
For software agents, choosing the right skill gets harder as the library
grows \cite{chen2026skillscaling}. We found no robotics study that measures
plan success against library size, removes skills one at a time, or swaps a
classical and a learned version of the same skill under one planner.

\paragraph{Reproducible evaluation}
Learning results swing with seeds and evaluation choices
\cite{agarwal2021precipice,gorsane2022evaluation}, and agent benchmarks now
report run-to-run variance rather than remove it \cite{yao2024taubench,bjarnason2026randomness}. Caching and replaying model
calls is established for software agents \cite{aisi2024inspect,chawla2026chronicle}, but a cache keyed on the prompt only works if everything
upstream of the prompt is deterministic. Robot simulators document
determinism only for one version on one machine, and some vary across
parallel environments \cite{mujocodocs,isaaclabdocs,zhang2026gpusimbench}. We found no embodied benchmark in which whole episodes, model
calls included, replay byte for byte.

\section{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{The fleet and missions}
A mixed fleet of fixed-wing aircraft and multirotors at one depot delivers
medical supplies to clinics and rooftops. Fixed-wing aircraft fly long legs
and drop packages by parachute; multirotors serve short legs and rooftops,
where they land. Requests arrive in plain language (urgent and routine
deliveries, cancellations, clinics closing for a time), and events (a
vehicle fails, a zone closes, the wind changes) arrive from a scripted
stream. \stub{Distances, fleet sizes, and request rates, based on published
operations.}

\paragraph{Skills}
A skill is an autonomy plugin with a short description and typed parameters,
for example \texttt{drop(clinic, item)} or \texttt{land(site)}. Each
vehicle runs one skill at a time over its own controller and dynamics. Some
skills have both a classical and a learned version behind the same
interface. Each vehicle comes with a card of its limits (minimum speed, turn
radius, whether it can hover, endurance), given to the planner as text, as
numbers, or not at all.

\paragraph{The planner}
When a request or event arrives, the planner sees the open requests, each
vehicle's reported state, and the skill descriptions, and returns one skill
call per vehicle. The calls are parsed and checked against the parameter
types; a call a vehicle cannot run is counted and refused. A separation
guard keeps vehicles apart.

\paragraph{Tasks}
Every delivery task also has a structured form, so the optimizer and the
auction plan from the same information as the language model, and a parser
is the reference on requests that fit a template. To test whether published
results hold under replay, we also re-run the coverage and formation tasks
of Web-of-Drones with its published prompts \cite{iannoli2026webofdrones},
with and without fixed-wing aircraft in the fleet.

\paragraph{Scoring}
Each mission type has one primary score, fixed in advance and computed by
code. Capability violations and separation losses are reported beside it.
Intervals are bootstrapped over episodes, and paired comparisons use the
same episodes for every planner.

\section{Ripple}
\label{sec:system}

Ripple began as a Rust port of SCRIMMAGE \cite{demarco2018}. Four
properties make it suited to growing a skill library and comparing planners
on it.

\paragraph{Skills are plugins}
Every behaviour is a plugin with a parameter struct, registered in one line
and selected in a mission file. Typed ports check units and frames between a
skill, its controller, and its vehicle when a mission loads, so one skill can
drive vehicles of different kinds, and a classical and a learned version of
a skill are interchangeable. Fixed-wing aircraft on a 6-DOF model and
multirotors fly in the same world. \stub{Lines of code per skill in the
library.}

\paragraph{Determinism}
Each tick runs fixed phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:tick}), and output is
byte-identical at any number of workers. Every random number is keyed by
what it is for (seed, agent, plugin, tick, quantity), so two planners on the
same episode meet the same wind and the same events, and, with the
planner's calls cached by prompt, an experiment reruns byte for byte without
contacting a model.

\paragraph{Speed}
\prov A 60~s mission with three Rascals on the 6-DOF model under their
autopilots, at 100 steps per second, runs in about 40~ms on one core of an
Apple M2 Pro, including writing the recording. Sweeps over any mission
parameter and seed are built in, and run in parallel shards.

\paragraph{Checked models}
Each model is checked against an independent implementation. Over 100
generated scenarios Ripple agrees with SCRIMMAGE to $2.8\times10^{-11}$~m,
and the fixed-wing aircraft and its autopilot agree to
$2.3\times10^{-13}$~m. This shows the equations and timing came through
intact, not that they match a real aircraft.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Setup}
\stub{Missions, fleet, skills, planners, numbers of episodes.} Planners are
several pinned language models from at least two commercial families and
one open-weights model, a ReAct agent, the prompting schemes of SMART-LLM
and HMAS-2 \cite{kannan2024smartllm,chen2024scalable}, an allocation
optimizer, and an auction. Prompts are tuned on development scenarios only,
and the test scenarios, written first, are run once with frozen prompts; we
report how many prompt versions were tried. Planner calls are cached, so all
results rerun exactly.

\subsection{How much of a planner's score is noise?}
We check that every episode gives the same bytes at 1, 2, and 8 workers and
with visualization on or off. We then query the models live several times on
the same episodes and split the spread in scores into the model's own
variation and the world's, using the keyed random numbers to hold one fixed
while the other varies. \stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{Do planners know what each vehicle can do?}
On mixed fleets, we count calls that give a vehicle a task its dynamics rule
out, as the share of fixed-wing aircraft grows, for each planner and with
the vehicle cards absent, in text, or in numbers. Within one class of
airframe, adding dynamic detail barely changed outcomes in our trials;
between classes it decides what a vehicle can do at all. \stub{Results
pending.}

\subsection{How does success change with the skill library?}
We grow the library one skill at a time, remove skills one at a time, and
replace a classical skill with its learned twin with everything else held
fixed, measuring the primary score beside the lines of code each skill
took. We also vary how skills are described (full, terse, misleading,
missing) and how coarse they are, from raw waypoints to whole behaviours.
\stub{Results pending.}

\subsection{Where does a language model earn its cost?}
On the same episodes with scripted events, we rank every planner, with
intervals and the probability that one beats another. Requests either fit a
template, where a parser and the optimizer are the reference, or do not
(``clinic D's generator failed; move its vaccines to E before they
spoil''). We expect the optimizer to win on the first and the model to be
needed on the second \cite{choudhury2020scoba}. \stub{Results pending.}

\section{Determinism, Cost, and Reproduction}
\label{app:self}

A run gives the same bytes on 1, 2, or 8 workers, with recording and
tracing on or off, and whether the scenario is written in YAML or in Rust.
Between Linux on amd64 and macOS on arm64, events and summaries match
exactly, and trajectories differ by at most $2.4\times10^{-12}$~m.

Large fleets stay cheap. Ripple routes messages by subscriber: when each of
4{,}096 aircraft sends a message, the step takes 9.4~ms, against 378~ms in
SCRIMMAGE. A spatial grid makes collision checks among 4{,}096 agents 2.4
times faster than testing every pair, with the same result.

To reproduce every check in this paper, clone Ripple next to a SCRIMMAGE
checkout and run
\begin{verbatim}
python3 reference/suite.py --release --retain
\end{verbatim}
\texttt{reference/README.md} describes each check.
